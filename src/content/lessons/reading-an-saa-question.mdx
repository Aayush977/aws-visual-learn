## 58%, and not one unfamiliar service

Brightwell's platform lead sat his first SAA mock on a Sunday evening and scored 58%. He went
through the 27 he had got wrong expecting to find a service he had never met.

There wasn't one. He knew what every service in every wrong answer did. He could have written a
paragraph on each. On question after question he had picked an option that **worked** — it would
have run, it would have served traffic, it would not have fallen over — and it was still marked
incorrect, because a different option worked *and* satisfied a sentence he had skimmed past.

One question described a batch job that could be interrupted and restarted. He chose On-Demand
instances behind an Auto Scaling group. Correct answer: Spot. Another described a company that
must not change its application code. He chose "refactor the application to run on Lambda".

Here is what he was actually being told, twenty-seven times:

> The Cloud Practitioner exam asks whether you know what a service is. The Solutions Architect
> exam gives you four services you already know, all of which would work, and asks which one
> the **constraints in the paragraph** permit. It is a reading test with an AWS vocabulary.

He had spent six weeks studying services. He had spent zero minutes studying scenarios. Those
are different skills, and only one of them is on the paper.

## What the exam actually is

<Callout type="exam" title="SAA-C03 at a glance">
<strong>65 questions in 130 minutes.</strong> Only <strong>50 are scored</strong> — the other 15
are unmarked questions AWS is trialling for future papers, and they are not identified. You are
scored 100–1,000 and <strong>720 passes</strong>. Multiple choice (one right of four) and
multiple response (two or more right of five or more). No penalty for a wrong answer, so never
leave one blank. AWS puts the target candidate at around one year of hands-on design experience.
</Callout>

Two consequences worth sitting with.

**You can get roughly 14 of the 50 scored questions wrong and still pass.** You do not need to
answer everything. You need to answer the ones you can read properly, and stop bleeding time on
the ones you cannot.

**Two minutes per question is not much.** A scenario paragraph runs 60 to 100 words, and the four
options run another 60. Reading everything twice is not affordable. Reading the *right* sentence
once is — which is the entire skill this lesson is about.

## The five questions

Every scenario is a business brief with the constraints buried in it. Ask these five, in this
order, and the architecture is largely chosen before you look at the options.

Notice what these five have in common: **every one of them is a number or a boundary the
business states, not a preference you hold.** That is why the method works. The exam writer put
those facts in the paragraph deliberately, because they are what makes exactly one option
correct. Nothing else in the paragraph is doing any work.

## Load-bearing words and decoration

Most of a scenario is set dressing. Learning to see through it is worth more marks than any
individual service.

| Decoration — ignore it | Load-bearing — circle it |
| --- | --- |
| The industry, the country, the company size | "must not lose more than *n* minutes of data" |
| "A solutions architect is designing…" | "must recover within *n* minutes" |
| "The application runs on Amazon EC2 instances" | "traffic increases 10× **every Friday at 18:00**" |
| "The team is small" *(usually — see below)* | "the job can be **interrupted and resumed**" |
| The name of the internal system | "**without modifying the application**" |
| "The company is growing rapidly" | "data must **remain in** the EU" |
| | "must be **unreachable from the internet**" |
| | "accessed **once or twice a year**" |
| | "retrieval within **12 hours** is acceptable" |

Two of these deserve unpacking, because they are where marks go missing.

**"Without modifying the application"** is the single most under-read phrase on the exam. It
eliminates every answer containing the words *rewrite*, *refactor*, *re-architect*, or
*migrate to a different database engine* — no matter how much better that answer would be. If a
question says the application uses a POSIX file system and cannot be changed, the answer is EFS
or FSx, and "store the files in S3" is wrong even though S3 is cheaper and you would obviously
use S3 in real life.

**"The team is small"** or "the company has no operations staff" flips from decoration to
load-bearing the moment it appears, because it is the exam telling you to prefer the managed
service. It is the setup for the tie-breaker in the final sentence.

## The last sentence picks the winner

Here is the structural fact that changes how you sit the paper: **on a well-written SAA question,
all four options are usually technically functional.** They will all run. The wrong ones violate
a stated constraint, and when none of them does, the qualifier in the final sentence separates
them.

Find that qualifier before you read a single option.

## One question, taken apart

> *A company runs a content management application on a fleet of Amazon EC2 instances behind an
> Application Load Balancer. The application writes user-uploaded documents to a local directory
> and expects a standard file system path. Users report that a document uploaded through one
> session is missing when they return later. The company cannot modify the application code.
> Which solution meets these requirements with the LEAST operational overhead?*

Work it in order, and never touch the options until the end.

**Lost?** Uploaded documents. They are currently being lost, which is the reported symptom — so
durability is the actual problem, not performance.

**Down?** Not mentioned. No RTO. Ignore anything about failover.

**Boundary?** Not mentioned either. Nobody said Region, nobody said AZ failure. Resist adding
Multi-Region: the exam does not award marks for generosity.

**Load?** Not mentioned. Nothing about spikes. Auto Scaling is not the subject.

**Allowed?** Not mentioned.

**The constraint that decides it:** *"expects a standard file system path"* plus *"cannot modify
the application code."* Together these eliminate S3 completely — S3 is object storage reached
over an API, and using it requires changing the code. That is the trap, and it is baited with
the answer you would give in real life.

**The qualifier:** LEAST operational overhead. So between the two remaining file-system answers,
take the managed one.

The answer is **Amazon EFS mounted on every instance** — a shared POSIX file system, so any
instance serves any session, and AWS runs it. The distractors will be: store the documents in S3
(violates "cannot modify"), attach an EBS volume to each instance (EBS attaches to one instance,
so the documents stay stranded exactly as they are now), and run a self-managed NFS server on
EC2 (works, but you now patch and back up an NFS server — that is the operational overhead the
question told you to minimise).

<Callout type="gotcha">
Notice that the diagnosis never required knowing anything obscure. It required noticing that
<strong>"expects a standard file system path"</strong> was doing work, and that
<strong>"cannot modify the application code"</strong> outranked your instinct. Both sentences
were in plain sight. This is what "reading test" means.
</Callout>

## The five habits that convert this into marks

1. **Read the last sentence first.** The qualifier tells you what the four options differ on, so
   you read them looking for one thing instead of four.
2. **Extract the constraints before you look at the options.** Once you have read four plausible
   answers, they contaminate your reading of the paragraph — you start hunting for justification
   instead of constraints.
3. **Eliminate on violations, not on preference.** Do not ask "which is best?". Ask "which three
   break something the company said?" It is a faster question and it has a defensible answer.
4. **Never over-deliver.** Multi-Region when nobody mentioned a Region, encryption in transit
   when nobody mentioned compliance, active/active when the RTO is four hours — all wrong, and
   all wrong in the same way: they cost money the scenario did not authorise.
5. **Flag and move at 90 seconds.** You can afford 14 wrong out of 50. You cannot afford four
   minutes on one question and then rushing six others at the end.

Brightwell's platform lead re-sat a fresh mock a fortnight later and scored 84%, having learned,
by his own count, two new services in the interval. Nothing about his AWS knowledge had
meaningfully changed. What changed was that he stopped reading each scenario as a description of
a system and started reading it as **a list of things the company had told him he was not
allowed to do**.

That is the reframe the whole exam turns on, and it survives the exam. A real architecture brief
arrives in exactly the same shape: several paragraphs of context, a handful of load-bearing
constraints buried in it, and four approaches that would all technically work. The job — on the
paper and afterwards — is not knowing the most services. It is noticing which sentence is the
one you are not permitted to break.
