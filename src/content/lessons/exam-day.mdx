## The thing itself

Most of the anxiety around this exam is not about AWS. It is about not knowing how the exam
works — what the questions look like, whether a wrong answer costs you, what happens if you run
out of time. All of that is published, and none of it is frightening once it is written down.

Every figure below comes from AWS's own exam guide for **CLF-C02**, the current version.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Questions** | 65 |
| **Time** | 90 minutes |
| **Scoring** | Scaled score from **100 to 1,000** |
| **Pass mark** | **700** |
| **Unscored questions** | **15** of the 65 — you are not told which |
| **Cost** | 100 USD |
| **Format** | Multiple choice (one correct of four) and multiple response (two or more correct of five or more) |
| **Where** | A Pearson VUE test centre, or online with a remote proctor |
| **Valid for** | 3 years |

## Read the scoring twice, it matters

This is the part that changes how people sit the exam, and almost nobody has it explained to
them.

**Only 50 of the 65 questions count.** Fifteen are unscored — AWS is trialling them for future
exams — and they are not marked or identified in any way. You cannot spot them, and a question
you find bewildering may well be one of them.

**Your score is scaled, not a percentage.** It runs from 100 to 1,000, and 700 is the pass.
Because it is scaled rather than a raw count, 700 does not correspond to exactly 70% of
questions — the scaling compensates for slight differences in difficulty between exam versions.
As a rough working target, aiming to be confident on about **75%** of questions gives you
comfortable margin.

**There is no penalty for a wrong answer.** An unanswered question and a wrong answer score
identically: zero. This has one absolute consequence.

<Callout type="exam" title="Never leave a question blank. Ever.">
A guess has at worst a <strong>25% chance</strong> on a four-option question, and considerably
better once you have eliminated one or two options. A blank has a <strong>0% chance</strong>.
There is no situation, on any AWS exam, in which leaving an answer empty is correct — so if the
clock is nearly out, spend the last thirty seconds putting <em>something</em> in every empty box
before you do anything else.
</Callout>

## Where the questions come from

The exam guide publishes exactly how the 50 scored questions are distributed. Weight your
revision accordingly — this is public information and people still revise evenly across topics.

| Domain | Weight | Lessons here |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1 — Cloud Concepts** | **24%** | What Cloud Computing Actually Is · Cloud Economics · Well-Architected · Migration and the CAF |
| **2 — Security and Compliance** | **30%** | Shared Responsibility · How IAM Decides · Encryption · Threat Detection · Governance |
| **3 — Cloud Technology and Services** | **34%** | Global Infrastructure · Compute · Storage · Databases · Networking · AI and Analytics · Services to Recognise |
| **4 — Billing, Pricing, and Support** | **12%** | Pricing and Cost Management · EC2 Purchase Options · Support Plans |

Two things worth noticing. **Security is 30%** — nearly a third of the exam, and far more than
most beginners expect from an entry-level certification. And **Domains 2 and 3 together are
64%**, so if revision time is short, that is where it goes.

## The reading technique

Cloud Practitioner questions are not trying to trick you, but they are written to a formula,
and knowing the formula is worth marks.

A scenario question has three parts: some **context** you mostly do not need, a **constraint**
that decides the answer, and a **question** that tells you what kind of answer to give. The
constraint is usually one clause, often in the middle, and often the shortest part.

**The qualifier in the question is doing real work.** These words are not filler:

- **"MOST cost-effective"** — several options work; pick the cheapest that still meets the
  constraint. The very cheapest option is often deliberately ruled out by one clause.
- **"LEAST operational overhead"** or **"with minimal management"** — choose the managed or
  serverless option, even if it costs more per hour.
- **"MOST secure"** — choose the answer with the smallest blast radius: roles over keys,
  private over public, least privilege over convenience.
- **"Highly available"** — multiple Availability Zones. A bigger instance is never the answer.

<Callout type="gotcha" title="Choose TWO means exactly two">
Multiple-response questions state how many answers to select, and they are marked all-or-nothing
— there are no partial marks for getting one of two right. Select the exact number asked for,
no more and no less, and re-read the count before moving on. Miscounting is a pure gift of a
mark to nobody.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note" title="Afterwards">
You will normally see a pass or fail result on screen when you finish, with the official score
report following in your certification account within a few days. That report breaks your
performance down by domain, which is genuinely useful if you plan to go on to Solutions
Architect Associate. If it does not go your way, you can retake after <strong>14 days</strong>,
and there is no limit on attempts — plenty of people pass on the second sitting having lost
nothing but a fortnight.
</Callout>

One last reframing, because it takes some of the weight off.

You are not being asked to prove you could build anything. Cloud Practitioner is a
**recognition** exam: it asks whether you know what the services are for, who is responsible for
what, and roughly what things cost. Every lesson on this site has been pointed at exactly that.

If you have read them and can hold your own on the ten confusable pairs, you are closer than
the nerves suggest. Book the date.
