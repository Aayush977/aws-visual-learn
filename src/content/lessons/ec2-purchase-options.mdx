An `m5.large` in London is one machine, but AWS will sell it to you at roughly five different
prices. The instance is identical in every case — same CPU, same memory, same performance.
What differs is **what you promised in exchange for the discount**.

There are only two currencies you can pay with:

1. **Certainty about time** — "I will be using this much compute for the next year or three."
2. **Tolerance of interruption** — "Take it away whenever you need it back."

Every pricing model is one of those, or neither, or a special case about licensing.

## On-Demand — the one with no strings

You pay per second (with a 60-second minimum for Linux), you start and stop whenever you
want, and you have promised nothing. It is the most expensive per hour and the right answer
surprisingly often.

Reach for it when the workload is **short-lived, unpredictable, or brand new**. You cannot
sensibly commit to three years of an instance size you picked last Tuesday, and a development
environment that runs eight hours a day is already getting a two-thirds discount by simply
being switched off at night.

<Callout type="cost">
The cheapest instance is the one that is not running. Before reaching for any discount, ask
whether the thing needs to be on at all — turning off development and test environments
outside working hours is a bigger saving than most Reserved Instance purchases, and it
requires no commitment whatsoever.
</Callout>

## Savings Plans — commit to money, not to machines

This is the modern default for steady workloads. You commit to spending a certain amount per
hour on compute — say £4/hour — for **one or three years**, and everything up to that amount
is billed at a heavily discounted rate. Anything above it falls back to On-Demand pricing.

There are two kinds, and the difference is examinable:

| | **Compute Savings Plans** | **EC2 Instance Savings Plans** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Discount | Up to ~66% | Up to ~72% |
| Applies to | EC2, **Fargate and Lambda** too | EC2 only |
| You may change | Instance family, size, OS, Region — anything | Size and OS, within one family in one Region |
| Choose it when | You expect your architecture to change | You are certain of the family and Region |

You also choose how much to pay up front — **All Upfront** (biggest discount), **Partial
Upfront**, or **No Upfront** (smallest discount, but still a large one, and no cash needed
on day one).

<Callout type="exam">
If a question says the workload is steady but the team "may migrate to containers or
serverless", the answer is a **Compute Savings Plan** — it is the only commitment that also
covers Fargate and Lambda. If the question stresses maximum discount on a known, fixed
instance family, it is an **EC2 Instance Savings Plan** or a Reserved Instance.
</Callout>

## Reserved Instances — the older way to do the same thing

Reserved Instances predate Savings Plans and reach a similar discount, but you commit to a
specific instance family in a specific Region for one or three years. Two flavours:

- **Standard RIs** — the deepest discount, but you can only change the size within the family.
- **Convertible RIs** — a smaller discount in exchange for the right to exchange them for a
  different family, OS or tenancy later.

Both can be sold on the **Reserved Instance Marketplace** if your plans change, which Savings
Plans cannot. That aside, for new commitments a Savings Plan is usually the better instrument,
and AWS's own guidance says so.

## Spot — up to 90% off, if you can take a punch

AWS always has capacity it has not sold. Rather than let it idle, it sells it at a steep
discount on the condition that it can take it back with **two minutes' notice** when a paying
On-Demand customer wants it.

That two-minute warning arrives as an interruption notice your application can listen for —
enough time to checkpoint work, drain connections, and exit cleanly.

**Good Spot workloads** share one property: an interrupted unit of work can simply be run
again.

- Batch processing, ETL jobs, nightly report generation
- CI/CD build agents
- Video rendering and transcoding farms
- Machine-learning training with checkpointing
- Stateless web servers, *if* they sit behind a load balancer with On-Demand capacity as well

**Bad Spot workloads** are the ones where an interruption is a real event: a database, a
license server, anything holding state in memory that cannot be reconstructed, or a job with
a hard deadline and no time to retry.

<Callout type="gotcha">
Spot is not an auction any more, and it is not "cheap because it is slower". It is the same
hardware at the same performance — you are being paid to accept the risk of eviction. The
common mistake is running a stateful application on Spot to save money and discovering what
"reclaimed" means at 2 a.m.
</Callout>

## Dedicated Hosts and Dedicated Instances

Both give you hardware that no other AWS customer is on. The difference is how much visibility
you get:

- **Dedicated Instance** — isolated hardware, but AWS decides which physical server. Billed
  per instance.
- **Dedicated Host** — an actual physical server allocated to you, with visible sockets and
  cores. Billed per host, whether or not you fill it.

You want a Dedicated Host almost exclusively for one reason: **software licensed per physical
socket or core** — Windows Server, SQL Server, Oracle — where the licence requires you to
prove which physical hardware it ran on. A minority of compliance regimes also require it.

<Callout type="exam">
"Bring your own licence", "per-socket licensing", "must run on dedicated physical hardware
for compliance" → <strong>Dedicated Hosts</strong>. If the question is about performance or
cost, it is never Dedicated Hosts — they are the most expensive option on the board.
</Callout>

  who="A visual-effects studio rendering frames for television"
  problem="Rendering runs in enormous overnight bursts — three thousand instance-hours between 7pm and 6am, and almost nothing during the day.   solution="A small On-Demand baseline runs the job scheduler and the asset database. The render fleet moves to a Spot Fleet spread across six instance types in three Availability Zones. The property that made this work was not the studio's cleverness — it was that **a frame is
independent**. Ask that question first about any candidate for Spot: if one unit of work is
lost, can it simply be done again?
