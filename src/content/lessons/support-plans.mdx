## 2am on a Saturday

Kettle & Fox went down at two in the morning on the first Saturday in December — the best
trading weekend of their year.

They were on the **Developer** support plan, which had been chosen the way most support plans
are chosen: it was the cheapest one that was not free, and nobody expected to need it. What
Developer entitles you to is email, during business hours, from one nominated contact.

So at 2am they sent an email. The reply arrived on Monday morning, courteous and helpful, by
which point the weekend had happened without them.

The second half of the story is the one people miss. They also had a televised advertising slot
booked for January — twenty times normal traffic, arriving inside ten minutes — and no way to
have anyone at AWS look at the plan beforehand, raise their service quotas in advance, or stand
by while it went out.

Both problems were the same problem, and it was not technical: **they had bought the wrong rung
of a ladder, months earlier, on a day when nothing was wrong.**

## The ladder

This is one of the few genuinely list-shaped topics on the Cloud Practitioner exam, and there
is no clever framing that removes the need to know it. The good news is that the plans form a
ladder, each rung adding to the one below, and only about four facts actually get tested.

## The four facts that actually get tested

Almost every support-plan question turns on one of these:

**1. Who may open a technical case?**
Basic cannot open technical cases at all — only billing and account ones. Every paid plan can.

**2. When is the phone available?**
Developer is **email only, business hours**. **Business** and above get **24/7 phone, chat and
email**. If a scenario mentions needing to phone someone at 3am, Developer is eliminated.

**3. How fast, for a system that is down?**

| Plan | Production system down | Business-critical system down |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Developer | *not offered* | *not offered* |
| **Business** | **&lt; 1 hour** | *not offered* |
| **Enterprise On-Ramp** | &lt; 1 hour | **&lt; 30 minutes** |
| **Enterprise** | &lt; 1 hour | **&lt; 15 minutes** |

**4. Who gets a Technical Account Manager?**
Only the top two. **Enterprise On-Ramp** gets a **pool** of TAMs; **Enterprise** gets a
**designated** TAM — an assigned individual. Business and below get none.

<Callout type="exam">
<strong>Business</strong> is the answer to most support questions, because it is the cheapest
plan a real production workload can sit on: 24/7 phone access, unlimited contacts, a
&lt;1 hour response when production is down, and the full Trusted Advisor check set. Reach past
it only when the question mentions a <strong>Technical Account Manager</strong>, a
<strong>&lt;15 or &lt;30 minute</strong> response, or a major launch needing hands-on help.
</Callout>

## Trusted Advisor, and where it changes

**AWS Trusted Advisor** inspects your live account and reports against best practice in five
categories:

- **Cost optimisation** — idle instances, unattached volumes, under-used reservations
- **Performance** — over-utilised instances, poor configuration
- **Security** — open security groups, exposed access keys, MFA on the root account
- **Fault tolerance** — resources without backups, workloads in a single AZ
- **Service limits** — quotas you are approaching

With **Basic** and **Developer** you get **seven core checks** — the security and service-limit
essentials. With **Business and above** you get the **full set**, plus programmatic access
through the API. That jump is a favourite exam detail.

<Callout type="gotcha">
Do not confuse the two halves of the <strong>AWS Health Dashboard</strong>.
<strong>Service health</strong> shows the general status of AWS services, publicly, for
everyone. <strong>Your account health</strong> shows events affecting <em>your specific
resources</em> — and it is available on every plan including Basic. Older material calls this
second one the "Personal Health Dashboard"; AWS retired that name, so treat the two as
question wording you might still meet rather than as two separate services.
</Callout>

## The extras at the top

Worth recognising by name:

- **Technical Account Manager (TAM)** — a named technical contact who knows your architecture,
  reviews it proactively, and escalates for you. The single defining feature of Enterprise.
- **Infrastructure Event Management (IEM)** — hands-on AWS help planning and running a major
  event: a product launch, a migration cutover, a Black Friday. Included with Enterprise, and
  available for a fee with Business.
- **Support Concierge** — a billing and account specialist team, from Enterprise On-Ramp up.
- **Well-Architected reviews with AWS** — included at the Enterprise tiers.

Kettle & Fox moved to **Business** on the Monday. The next incident was answered in eleven
minutes, and the January campaign ran with an AWS engineer already briefed, quotas already
raised, and somebody on the call who knew what was about to happen.

The rungs are worth memorising, but the useful idea underneath them is smaller than the table:
**a support plan is the only thing on your bill that you buy entirely for a day that has not
happened yet.** Every other line item does something for you this afternoon. This one does
nothing at all until two in the morning, and by then you cannot buy it retrospectively.

That is also why the exam questions read the way they do. When a scenario mentions the hour, the
day of the week, or a launch date, it is not scene-setting — it is telling you which rung.
