## The problem

Brightwell's claims system is a normal enterprise web application: a Java front end, business
logic behind it, an Oracle-shaped relational database underneath. It ran on three physical
servers in the data centre they were closing, and it cannot be rewritten — it is twelve years
old, it works, and the people who wrote it have gone.

This is the ordinary case, and worth saying plainly: **most real migrations are not serverless
rewrites.** They are applications like this one, moved as they are, onto an architecture that
gives them the resilience the data centre never did.

The requirements are unremarkable and completely typical. Survive losing a server. Survive
losing a whole Availability Zone. Do not let anything holding claim data be reachable from the
internet. Handle Monday morning, which is three times Wednesday afternoon.

## Why each piece, and not the obvious alternative

The single most examined confusion in this whole architecture:

**Public and private subnets** are the security spine of this design, and the distinction is
simpler than it looks: a subnet is public because **its route table sends `0.0.0.0/0` to an
internet gateway**. Nothing else makes it public — not its name, not its diagram colour. Only
the load balancer and the NAT gateways live in those. Everything holding claim data sits in
subnets with no such route, so there is no path from the internet to it at all.

**Security groups referencing each other** is the detail that separates people who have built
this from people who have read about it. The database's security group does not allow a range
of IP addresses — it allows **port 5432 from the application tier's security group**. Instances
come and go with scaling, their addresses change constantly, and the rule keeps working because
it names an identity rather than an address.

**One NAT gateway per Availability Zone.** Put a single NAT in one zone and you have built a
cross-zone dependency into an architecture whose entire purpose is surviving the loss of a zone
— plus you now pay to move traffic across zones. This is a favourite exam detail.

<Callout type="cost" title="The NAT gateway is the surprise on this bill">
Newcomers expect the instances or the database to dominate the cost. Frequently it is the
<strong>NAT gateways</strong>: charged per hour <em>and</em> per gigabyte processed, and there
are two of them for resilience.

The standard fix is a <strong>gateway VPC endpoint</strong> for S3 and DynamoDB. It costs
nothing, and it keeps that traffic off the NAT entirely — removing both the processing charge
and an internet round trip. If a question mentions reducing NAT costs, this is the answer.
</Callout>

## Roughly what it costs

This architecture has a **floor** — unlike the first two projects, it costs money at 3am
because things are running at 3am.

| Line | Rough monthly cost |
| --- | --- |
| Application Load Balancer | roughly $16–25 plus capacity units |
| 2 × NAT gateway | roughly $32 each, plus per-GB processing |
| EC2 instances (2 small, always on) | tens of pounds, less with a Savings Plan |
| RDS Multi-AZ (small instance) | roughly double single-AZ |
| **Floor before any traffic** | **well over £100/month** |

That floor is the honest trade for resilience, and it is why the serverless patterns are so
attractive for new work. It is also why **right-sizing** and **Savings Plans** matter here and
barely register in Projects 1 and 2.

Unlike the earlier projects, an ALB, two NAT gateways and a Multi-AZ database will spend real

This is the architecture the exam loves most, because almost every interesting requirement can
be expressed as a change to one box in it. *Users in Asia are slow* — put CloudFront in front.
*Reads are swamping the database* — add a read replica. *The batch job blocks the website* — put
an SQS queue and a worker tier beside it. *Costs too much at night* — schedule the Auto Scaling
group down.

Learn the diagram once and you are not memorising a hundred scenarios. You are looking at one
picture and deciding which box the question is pointing at.
