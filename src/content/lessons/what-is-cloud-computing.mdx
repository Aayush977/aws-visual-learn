## The Tuesday that broke Kettle & Fox

Kettle & Fox sell enamel mugs and wool blankets out of a converted mill in Yorkshire. Two
people, one website, and one server in a rack in Leeds that had taken every order since 2019
without once complaining. About 200 visitors on a good day.

On a Tuesday in November a presenter held up one of their mugs on daytime television and said
where she had bought it.

Nine minutes later the site was down. Not slow — down. The server was, technically, fine: it
was doing precisely what it had always done, just for twenty thousand people instead of two
hundred. Orders that would have paid for the year went nowhere, and the shop found out from
Twitter.

The part that stung came later. **The capacity they needed already existed.** It was racked,
powered, cooled and idle, two hundred miles away, owned by somebody else — and there was no way
on earth for Kettle & Fox to borrow six hours of it.

That gap, between capacity that exists and capacity you can actually get at, is the entire
subject of this website.

## Computers are lumpy

If you have never touched AWS, ignore the service names for a moment. Every one of them
exists to solve the problem Kettle & Fox hit, and that problem is much older than the cloud.

**Computers are lumpy.** You cannot buy 1.4 servers. You buy one, or two, and you buy them
weeks before you need them, and you keep paying for them long after the reason you bought
them has gone away. Cloud computing is the business of turning that lump into a dial.

<Callout type="analogy">
You do not buy a diesel generator to boil a kettle. You plug into the grid, turn the tap
of electricity up and down as you please, and at the end of the month you pay for exactly
what flowed through the meter. Nobody calls that "renting electricity" — it is just how
electricity works now. Cloud computing is that same shift, applied to computers.
</Callout>

Option A is the Tuesday that happened. Option B is the Tuesday that was available for about
eleven pounds.

Nothing in that story is about technology being clever. It is about **who holds the risk of
guessing wrong**. When you own the servers, you have to predict next year's traffic today,
and you pay for being wrong in either direction — outages if you under-guess, idle metal if
you over-guess. When you rent by the hour, the guess stops mattering. Kettle & Fox were not
punished for buying a bad server; they were punished for having to decide, in 2019, how famous
they would be in November 2026.

<Callout type="gotcha">
"The cloud is cheaper" is not quite the claim, and the exam is careful about this. A server
running flat out, 24 hours a day, for four years, is often *cheaper to own*. What the cloud
removes is the **cost of being wrong** — and almost no real workload is flat.
</Callout>

## The six advantages, and why each one is really the same idea

AWS publishes six "advantages of cloud computing", and the Cloud Practitioner exam quotes
them almost word for word. They are worth learning as a list, but they make far more sense
once you notice that four of them are just the story above, told from different angles.

The two that are genuinely separate ideas are worth a sentence each:

- **Economies of scale.** AWS runs millions of servers. Its cost per unit of compute, per
  gigabyte stored, per watt of cooling is far below what any single company can reach. Some
  of that saving is passed on — which is why AWS has cut prices well over a hundred times
  without anyone asking.
- **Go global in minutes.** Serving users in Sydney from a London server means every click
  crosses the planet twice. Owning a building in Sydney to fix that is a year-long project.
  On AWS it is a deployment into another Region, and you can undo it on Friday.

<Callout type="cost">
The capital-versus-variable distinction has a name the exam likes: **CapEx** (money spent up
front on assets you own) becomes **OpEx** (money spent on what you consumed this month). If
a question mentions "avoid large up-front investment" or "no upfront capital expenditure",
it is pointing at this advantage.
</Callout>

## The three service models: where the line is drawn

Every cloud service is a decision about how much of the stack somebody else operates. There
are only three named positions on that line, and once you can place a service on it you can
usually predict what it costs and what it stops you from doing.

A quick way to place any service you meet: **ask what you would have to patch.**

| If you must patch… | You are using | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The operating system | **IaaS** | EC2 |
| Nothing, but you deploy code | **PaaS** | Lambda, Elastic Beanstalk |
| Nothing, and you deploy nothing | **SaaS** | WorkMail, QuickSight |

<Callout type="exam">
Cloud Practitioner questions rarely say "IaaS" outright. They describe a requirement —
"the team wants full control over the operating system" (IaaS / EC2) or "the company wants
to run code without managing servers" (PaaS / Lambda) — and you have to recognise the shape.
</Callout>

## The three deployment models

A much shorter list, and it comes up mostly as a vocabulary check.

- **Cloud** — everything runs on AWS. A company founded after about 2010 is usually here.
- **Hybrid** — some workloads on AWS, some in your own data centre, connected together
  (typically with **AWS Direct Connect** or a **Site-to-Site VPN**). This is where most large
  established companies actually live, often for years.
- **On-premises** — everything in your own facility. Sometimes called "private cloud" when it
  is virtualised and self-service internally, which confuses people; the distinguishing
  feature is still that you own the hardware.

<Callout type="gotcha">
Hybrid is not a failure state or a halfway house someone forgot to finish. Regulated data
that legally cannot leave a country, a mainframe nobody dares move, or a factory floor that
must keep running when the internet drops — these are permanent, deliberate reasons to stay
hybrid.
</Callout>

This is the single most common reason a real company moves to AWS, and it is worth noticing
that the win came from the **shape** of their demand, not its size. A workload that is
genuinely flat around the clock is the one case where owning the hardware can still win.
</Scenario>

## What you are actually buying

Before the service-by-service lessons, one framing that makes the rest of this site easier.
AWS sells you four things, and almost every service is a variation on one of them:

1. **Compute** — somewhere to run code. *(EC2, Lambda, ECS)*
2. **Storage** — somewhere to keep bytes. *(S3, EBS, EFS)*
3. **Networking** — how the bytes and the requests move. *(VPC, Route 53, CloudFront)*
4. **Databases** — storage that understands the shape of your data. *(RDS, DynamoDB)*

Everything else — security, monitoring, billing tools — exists to control, watch or pay for
those four. When a new service name appears, asking "which of the four is this?" gets you
most of the way to understanding it.

Kettle & Fox needed one of each: somewhere to run the shop, somewhere to keep the product
photographs, something to spread the load across more than one machine, and somewhere to hold
the orders. You will build exactly that, piece by piece, over the next few lessons — and when
you reach **scaling and high availability**, you will build the thing that would have saved
their Tuesday.

One last thing about Kettle & Fox, because it is the reason this site exists.

Nothing that went wrong on their Tuesday was a hard technical problem. Nobody was hacked, no
code was wrong, no disk failed. They lost their best day of the year to a decision made in 2019
about how much computer to buy — a decision they were forced to make years before they could
possibly have known the answer.

**Everything else on this site is a variation on removing that decision.** Auto Scaling removes
it for compute. S3 removes it for storage. Serverless removes it so thoroughly that there is
nothing left to size at all.

When a service name starts to feel like jargon, ask what guess it is saving you from. There is
almost always one.
