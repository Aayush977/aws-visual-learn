## The gap nobody warns you about

You can read every lesson on this site, pass the Cloud Practitioner exam, and still freeze the
first time somebody sits you in front of the AWS console. It has hundreds of services, most
screens assume you already know the vocabulary, and there is a genuine fear underneath it all
that clicking the wrong thing will cost money.

That fear is reasonable and it is also the thing that keeps people at the reading stage for
months.

So this lesson is different from every other lesson here. There is almost no theory in it. You
are going to open a real AWS account, protect it properly, put an actual web page on the
internet from an actual S3 bucket, and then delete everything you made.

Half an hour. At the end of it, "I have used AWS" becomes a true sentence about you.

<Callout type="gotcha" title="Read this before you start">
Two honest warnings. <strong>First</strong>, opening an account requires a debit or credit card
— AWS takes a small temporary authorisation (typically about $1, refunded) to verify it is
real. That is normal and unavoidable. <strong>Second</strong>, everything in this lesson stays
inside the always-free limits, but the account is <em>capable</em> of spending money, which is
why the very first thing you will do is set up a spending alarm. Do not skip that step and do
not skip the teardown at the end.
</Callout>

## Step 1 — The account, and the safety net

AWS changed how new accounts work in 2025, and most tutorials you will find online are still
describing the old system. Here is the current arrangement:

- New accounts start on a **Free plan** with **$100 of credits immediately**, and up to **$100
  more** as you complete some getting-started activities — up to **$200 over six months**.
- The Free plan **ends after six months, or when the credits run out**, whichever happens first.
- **Over 30 services are always free** within monthly limits, on both plans, and that never
  expires.
- On the Free plan you are **not charged** unless you deliberately convert to a Paid plan.

<Callout type="exam" title="The exam may still describe the old model">
Older practice questions and study guides refer to a <strong>12-month free tier</strong> and a
three-part structure of <em>always free · 12 months free · short trials</em>. That was the
arrangement for accounts created before the change. If an exam question uses that framing,
answer within it — but know that what you are about to sign up for works as described above.
Always check <a href="https://aws.amazon.com/free/">the AWS Free Tier page</a> for the current
terms, because AWS has changed them before and may again.
</Callout>

<Callout type="gotcha" title="Why MFA on the root user is the one non-negotiable">
The root user is the email address you just signed up with, and it can do <em>absolutely
anything</em> to this account, including running up an enormous bill and locking you out. It
cannot be restricted by any policy. Automated bots scan for AWS credentials constantly — the
<em>How IAM Decides</em> lesson tells the true story of a leaked key that was mining
cryptocurrency in four Regions within nine minutes. An authenticator app makes a stolen
password useless on its own.
</Callout>

## Step 2 — Prove who you are, from a terminal

Before building anything, one command — because it makes the whole permissions model concrete
in a way no diagram can.

**CloudShell** is a terminal that runs inside the console. Nothing to install, nothing to
configure, no cost. Click the terminal icon `>_` in the top navigation bar and wait about
twenty seconds for it to start.

Then type:

```bash
aws sts get-caller-identity
```

You will get back something like:

```json
{
    "UserId": "123456789012",
    "Account": "123456789012",
    "Arn": "arn:aws:iam::123456789012:root"
}
```

Three facts just arrived. Your **account number**. Who you currently are — and note that it
says `root`, which is why you set up MFA. And your **ARN**, the Amazon Resource Name, which is
the addressing format AWS uses for every single thing that exists in it.

<Callout type="note" title="One command worth keeping">
<code>aws sts get-caller-identity</code> answers "who am I actually right now?", and it solves
more permission confusion than any other command, because the answer is very often "a different
identity than you assumed". Professionals run it constantly.
</Callout>

## Step 3 — Put a page on the internet

Now the part that makes it real. You are going to serve a web page out of **S3** — object
storage, the service more AWS things touch than any other.

First, make the page. In CloudShell:

```bash
echo '<!doctype html><meta charset="utf-8">
<title>I built this</title>
<h1>This page is served from Amazon S3.</h1>
<p>I made it on my first day.</p>' > index.html
```

<Callout type="gotcha" title="You just did the thing everyone gets fired for — correctly">
Public S3 buckets are among the most reported causes of data breaches on AWS, and other lessons
here will tell you to keep buckets private. Both things are true, and the difference is the
whole of the <em>Shared Responsibility Model</em> in one decision.

A bucket holding a <strong>public website</strong> should be public. That is what the data is
for. A bucket holding customer records, exported orders or backups should never be, and the
disasters happen when somebody makes the second kind public to fix a problem quickly and then
forgets.

The mechanism is identical. Only the intent differs — and AWS cannot know your intent, which is
precisely why it is your half of the responsibility and it made you click through a warning.
</Callout>

## Step 4 — Take it down

The most important professional habit on this entire site, and the one tutorials skip.

Anything you leave running keeps costing money for as long as it exists. A forgotten resource
does not remind you it is there; it just quietly appears on a bill in five weeks. Getting into
the habit of destroying what you built — every time, immediately — is what separates people who
experiment freely from people who are afraid to touch anything.

<Callout type="cost" title="The habit, stated once">
Build it, look at it, delete it. Every experiment, every time, in the same sitting.

Almost every "AWS charged me hundreds of pounds" story on the internet is the same story: a
thing was created to try something out, it worked, and nobody deleted it. There is no cleanup
fairy — the meter runs until you turn the resource off, and the invoice arrives weeks later.
</Callout>

## What you now know that you cannot get from reading

You have an account, protected by MFA, watched by a budget alarm. You have run a command
against the AWS API and seen your own ARN come back. You have created a bucket in the global
namespace, made a deliberate decision about public access, served a page to the internet, and
destroyed it cleanly.

That is a real, if small, piece of cloud work — and it maps onto four things the exam tests:

| What you did | What it is called |
| --- | --- |
| Signed up, chose Basic support, set a budget | **Billing, pricing and support** — Domain 4 |
| Enabled MFA on the root user | **Access management** — Domain 2 |
| Decided a bucket should be public | **Shared responsibility model** — Domain 2 |
| Created and deleted an S3 bucket | **Storage services** — Domain 3 |

Every remaining lesson on this site will make more sense now, because you have somewhere to put
what you read. When a lesson mentions a bucket, a Region, an ARN or the root user, you have
touched all four.

<Callout type="note" title="What to try next, when you are ready">
Nothing here is required, and all of it stays within always-free limits if you tear down
afterwards. In rough order of how much they teach:
<br />· Create an <strong>IAM user</strong> for yourself with <code>AdministratorAccess</code>,
and stop using the root user entirely. This is what every professional does on day one.
<br />· Write a two-line <strong>Lambda</strong> function in the console and run it. Serverless
compute, no server anywhere in sight.
<br />· Launch a <code>t3.micro</code> <strong>EC2 instance</strong>, connect with Session
Manager rather than SSH keys, and terminate it within the hour.
</Callout>

There is a moment, somewhere around the point the website URL loads, where the cloud stops
being an abstraction. It is a genuinely small thing — one HTML file in a bucket — and it does
not matter at all that it is small.

What matters is that the console is no longer a place you are frightened of clicking in. Every
lesson from here reads differently once you have somewhere to try it.
