## The price that would not change

Kettle & Fox put the enamel mugs into a sale at nine o'clock on a Monday. The price changed in
the database, the page was redeployed, and the developer who shipped it refreshed and saw
£14.00. Correct.

The marketing lead, sitting four feet away on the same wifi, saw £22.00. She refreshed. Still
£22.00. She refreshed eleven more times over twenty minutes, which is the single least useful
thing anybody did that morning.

By ten past, the picture was genuinely strange. Customers in Leeds were seeing the new price.
Customers in Bristol were not. One person saw the new price on their phone and the old one on
their laptop, in the same room, at the same second. Somebody said the word "caching" and
somebody else said "let's just redeploy", and they redeployed, and nothing changed.

Nothing was broken. **Two entirely separate caches were involved, each with its own clock,
and nobody in the room could say which one was lying.** By half past they had guessed at
three fixes and had no idea which of them had eventually worked.

## Two caches, two clocks

Here is the thing worth taking out of that morning, and it is the whole lesson:

> Between a user and your content sit **two independent caches** — the DNS answer and the
> object itself. They expire on different clocks, they are fixed in completely different
> ways, and the symptoms look identical from a browser.

Everything below is how each one decides what to keep, how long to keep it, and what actually
forces it to let go. Play the diagram first: the two caches are steps 1–2 and step 4, and the
reason Bristol and Leeds disagreed is that they were talking to different copies of both.

## Route 53, and what a record actually is

**Amazon Route 53** is AWS's DNS service. The "53" is the port DNS runs on, which is the kind
of joke you only get to make once.

A **hosted zone** is a domain you control — `example.com` — and inside it you create
**records**. A record is a name, a type, a value and a TTL. That is genuinely all it is:

```
shop.example.com.   A       300    203.0.113.10
```

Name, type, TTL in seconds, value. The whole of DNS is that line, repeated a few billion times.

### The record types worth knowing

- **A** — a name to an IPv4 address. The common one.
- **AAAA** — a name to an IPv6 address. Same job, longer addresses.
- **CNAME** — a name to *another name*. `www.example.com` → `shop.example.com`, and the
  resolver then has to look that one up too.
- **ALIAS** — Route 53's own invention. Looks like a CNAME, behaves like an A record, and
  points at an **AWS resource** — a CloudFront distribution, a load balancer, an S3 website
  endpoint, another record in the same zone.
- **MX** — where email for this domain goes. **TXT** — free text, which is how domain
  ownership and email anti-spoofing are proved. **NS** — which name servers are authoritative.

### The trap: you cannot CNAME the apex

The **zone apex** is the bare domain with nothing in front of it: `example.com`, not
`www.example.com`. And the DNS specification does not allow a CNAME there — an apex must carry
its zone's NS and SOA records, and a CNAME is not permitted to coexist with them.

So when someone puts a site behind CloudFront and wants `example.com` (not just
`www.example.com`) to reach it, they hit a wall. CloudFront hands you a name like
`d111111abcdef8.cloudfront.net`, not an address, so an A record has nothing to point at, and a
CNAME is refused at the apex.

**ALIAS is the answer, and it exists precisely for this.** It sits at the apex legally,
resolves to the AWS resource's current addresses, and follows them when they change.

<Callout type="exam">
If a question says <em>"the company wants users to reach the site at <code>example.com</code> as
well as <code>www.example.com</code>"</em> and the target is a CloudFront distribution or a load
balancer, the answer is an <strong>ALIAS record at the apex</strong>. Every option offering a
CNAME for the root domain is wrong on a technicality the exam loves.
</Callout>

### TTL, and why your change took an hour

The TTL is the promise you make to every resolver on the internet: *this answer is good for
this many seconds.* Once handed out, **you cannot take it back.** There is no button that
reaches into a resolver in Bristol and clears it.

That is the first half of the Kettle & Fox morning. Their records carried the default TTL, so
some resolvers were still inside a window that had started before the change and had every
right to keep answering with the old address.

The practical habit: **before a planned change, lower the TTL and wait.** Drop it to 60
seconds, wait for the old TTL to fully elapse so every resolver has picked up the short one,
then make the change — now the whole internet catches up in a minute. Raise it again
afterwards, because a long TTL is faster and cheaper the rest of the time.

<Callout type="gotcha">
A short TTL is not free. Every expiry is another query, which means more latency for users and
more Route 53 charges for you. A TTL is a trade between <em>how fast you can change your mind</em>
and <em>how much the steady state costs</em>. Lower it deliberately before a change, not
permanently out of anxiety.
</Callout>

### Routing policies and health checks, briefly

Covered in more detail in <a href={lessonUrl("request-lifecycle")}>How a Request Reaches Your
Application</a>,
but the shape worth carrying here: a record does not have to return one fixed answer.
**Simple** returns the same thing always. **Weighted** splits traffic by percentage, which is
how a canary release works. **Latency-based** returns whichever Region is fastest for that
user. **Failover** returns the backup only when a **health check** says the primary is down.
**Geolocation** answers differently per country.

Health checks are what make DNS-level failover possible at all — Route 53 probes the endpoint
and simply stops handing out an address that has stopped answering. It is also the only
mechanism here that can move traffic **between Regions**; a load balancer cannot.

## CloudFront, and what "cached" actually means

**CloudFront** is the CDN. Hundreds of edge locations, each keeping copies of your content, so
the mug photograph is served from a building in Sydney rather than crossing the Pacific.

The part people skip is *how the edge decides two requests are the same request.*

### The cache key

The edge stores objects under a **cache key**. By default that key is built from the
distribution, the path, and whatever you have told CloudFront matters — selected query strings,
headers and cookies.

That default is deliberate and worth understanding as a rule with two edges:

- **Include too little** and different content collides. Forward nothing, and
  `/products?id=1` and `/products?id=2` become the same cached object — one customer sees
  another's page. The bug reads as haunted and is a one-line configuration mistake.
- **Include too much** and the cache stops working. Forward every header and every cookie and
  each user's session cookie makes their request unique, so **every single request is a miss**.
  You now have a CDN that does nothing except add a hop, and a bill for it.

**Cache hit ratio** is the number to watch: the proportion of requests answered at the edge.
Low ratio on content that ought to be cacheable nearly always means the cache key is carrying
something it should not.

<Callout type="gotcha">
The most expensive version of this is forwarding <code>Cache-Control: no-cache</code> style
behaviour by accident — through a cookie, a <code>Vary</code> header, or an origin that stamps
every response as private. The distribution looks correctly configured and caches nothing.
Check the hit ratio before believing the console.
</Callout>

### How long an object stays

Three numbers, in priority order:

1. **What the origin says.** A `Cache-Control: max-age=3600` header on the response is the
   origin telling the edge how long it may keep this. Origin instructions win.
2. **The distribution's default TTL**, used when the origin says nothing at all.
3. **Minimum and maximum TTL**, which clamp the above — the floor and ceiling you are willing
   to accept regardless of what the origin claims.

The correct place to control caching is **the origin's headers**, because that keeps the
decision next to the content. A distribution-wide default is the blunt instrument you use
when you cannot change the origin.

### Invalidation, and the better habit

An **invalidation** tells every edge to drop an object now. It is the emergency exit: it takes
a few minutes to propagate, it is charged beyond a small monthly free allowance, and reaching
for it routinely is a sign the design is wrong.

The habit that removes the need for it is **versioned filenames**. Ship
`styles.a91f3c.css` rather than `styles.css`, and a deploy changes the *name*. A name the edge
has never seen cannot be stale — it is a guaranteed miss, fetched fresh, and the old object
simply ages out on its own. No invalidation, no waiting, no arguing about whose cache is wrong.

Keep the HTML on a short TTL, since it is the thing that references the new names, and give the
versioned assets a very long one. This is the standard static-site arrangement and it is what
Kettle & Fox should have had.

## S3 as an origin, and the bucket nobody should be able to read

The most common CloudFront origin is an S3 bucket, and there are two different ways to attach
one — which is the other thing people get wrong.

**The S3 website endpoint** is the one that supports index documents and redirect rules. It
speaks plain HTTP and it is, necessarily, public.

**The S3 REST endpoint** is the bucket's normal API address. It supports HTTPS and, crucially,
it can be locked so that **only your CloudFront distribution may read it**.

That lock is **Origin Access Control (OAC)**. CloudFront signs its requests to the bucket; a
bucket policy accepts requests from that distribution and nothing else. The bucket stays fully
private, every public request is refused, and the only route to the content is through
CloudFront — which is also where WAF, Shield, TLS and your cache behaviours live.

<Callout type="exam">
"Serve static content through CloudFront while keeping the S3 bucket private" is a standard
exam item, and the answer is <strong>Origin Access Control with a bucket policy that allows
only the distribution</strong>. Making the bucket public and putting CloudFront in front of it
is offered as a distractor in nearly every version of this question: it works, and it leaves
the bucket reachable directly, which defeats the point.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note" title="OAC replaced OAI">
Older material — and plenty of blog posts — say <strong>Origin Access Identity (OAI)</strong>.
That is the previous generation of the same idea. OAC is the current mechanism and supports
things OAI never did, including SSE-KMS encrypted objects. If you meet OAI in a question, it
means the same thing; if you are building something today, use OAC.
</Callout>

### Step by step

1. **Point a domain at Route 53 first.** The stack will not deploy without a hosted zone in the
   same account. Note the hosted zone id — it is a parameter.
2. **Deploy in `us-east-1`, and only there.** The repo says so in bold and it is not arbitrary:
   a certificate used by CloudFront must live in `us-east-1`, because CloudFront is a global
   service that reads its certificates from that one Region. Deploying to London will fail in a
   way that does not obviously say "wrong Region".
3. **Set `CreateApex` to `yes`.** It defaults to `no`, and the apex is the interesting half of
   this lesson. This one parameter is the difference between the lab confirming what you read
   and the lab skipping it.
4. **Wait.** Certificate validation and distribution deployment take a while. This is normal and
   is most of the hour.
5. **Open the site**, then open it again and compare. The second request is the cache.

### Now go and read the template

The deploy is the boring part. `templates/cloudfront-site.yaml` is the lesson, in about forty
lines:

- **`OriginAccessControl`** with `SigningBehavior: always` and `SigningProtocol: sigv4`. That is
  the mechanism from the S3-origin section above, written down: CloudFront signs every request
  to the bucket, and the bucket policy accepts nothing else.
- **`Route53RecordSetGroup`** and **`ApexRoute53RecordSetGroup`**. Look at the `Type` on both.
  They are **`A` records with an `AliasTarget`** — not CNAMEs. That is exactly the thing this
  lesson said a CNAME could never do at the apex, and here it is being done.
- **`HostedZoneId: 'Z2FDTNDATAQYW2'`** inside the alias target, with a comment saying it is
  always used for aliases pointing at CloudFront. It is a fixed constant, the same in every
  account in the world. Worth seeing once so it never confuses you.
- **`ResponseHeadersPolicy`**, which is how headers are added at the edge rather than by the
  origin.

<Callout type="gotcha" title="The README and the template disagree">
The README's prose says the distribution uses an <strong>origin access identity</strong>. The
template creates an <code>AWS::CloudFront::OriginAccessControl</code>. The code was modernised
to OAC and the prose was not updated — which is a real and useful lesson in itself:
<strong>in an AWS sample, the template is the truth and the README is a description of what the
template used to do.</strong> You will meet OAI in a lot of older writing; this is what the
replacement looks like.
</Callout>

### Break it on purpose

This is where the learning actually is. Change one thing, watch it, change it back.

1. **Make the bucket public and fetch it directly.** Then remove the public access and fetch the
   S3 URL again — `AccessDenied`, while the CloudFront URL keeps working. That gap is what OAC
   is for.
2. **Edit a file, upload it, and refresh.** It will not change, because the edge is still inside
   its TTL. Now you have reproduced the Kettle & Fox morning deliberately.
3. **Create an invalidation for `/*`** and refresh again. It changes. Note how long it took.
4. **Now do it the other way**: upload the new content under a *new filename* and point at that.
   Instant, no invalidation, nothing to wait for. That contrast is the argument for versioned
   filenames, and doing it once is worth more than reading it three times.
5. **Lower the record's TTL to 60, wait, then repoint it.** Watch how long the old answer
   survives. You cannot rush it, and feeling that is the point.

<Callout type="cost" title="Delete the stack when you are done">
Delete the CloudFormation stack, and empty the buckets it created first — CloudFormation will
refuse to delete a bucket with objects in it. The distribution itself costs little when idle,
but the hosted zone charges monthly whether you use it or not.
</Callout>

Kettle & Fox never did find out which of their three fixes worked, because they made all three
at once — and that is the real cost of not knowing how the edge works. It is not the forty
minutes of wrong prices. It is that they learned nothing from the forty minutes, and would
have been exactly as lost the next time.

The next sale, they dropped the record TTL the night before, gave the HTML sixty seconds and
the images a year, and put a hash in every asset filename. Nobody watched the prices at nine
o'clock, because there was nothing to watch.

A cache is not a mysterious force. It is a copy, held by somebody specific, for a length of
time you agreed to.
