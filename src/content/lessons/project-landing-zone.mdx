## The problem

Brightwell started, as almost everyone does, with **one AWS account**. Development, test and
production in the same place, separated by naming conventions and good intentions.

It works until it does not, and the failures are all the same shape:

- Somebody testing a cleanup script deletes production data, because nothing physically
  prevented it — only a prefix in a name.
- Nobody can say what the claims team spends, because every resource is in one bill and half of
  them are untagged.
- The security team wants to stop anyone using unapproved Regions. The only tool is IAM policies
  on hundreds of principals, and one missed policy defeats the whole thing.
- An auditor asks who can read production data and the honest answer takes three weeks.

Every one of those is the same underlying issue: **there is no boundary that a mistake cannot
cross**. IAM can express any rule you like, but IAM is also editable by anybody with sufficient
IAM permissions, which is a circular sort of protection.

An **account** is a different kind of boundary. Resources in one account are invisible to
another unless somebody deliberately builds a path. That is not a policy — it is the shape of
the platform.

## What Control Tower actually does

**AWS Control Tower** is not a new capability. Everything it builds could be assembled by hand
from Organizations, CloudTrail, Config, IAM Identity Center and a pile of CloudFormation. What
it provides is that assembly, done to AWS's recommended pattern, in an afternoon rather than a
quarter.

It sets up:

- An **Organization** with a recommended OU structure
- A **log archive** account and an **audit** account, separated from workloads on purpose
- **CloudTrail and Config** enabled organisation-wide, writing to the log archive
- **Guardrails** — preventive ones implemented as SCPs, detective ones as Config rules
- **IAM Identity Center** for human access
- **Account Factory**, so a new account arrives already governed rather than as a blank space
  somebody has to remember to configure

<Callout type="exam" title="Landing zone, Control Tower, Organizations">
Three terms that get used interchangeably and are not the same:
<br />· <strong>AWS Organizations</strong> — the underlying service. Accounts, OUs, consolidated
billing, SCPs.
<br />· <strong>Landing zone</strong> — the <em>design</em>: a well-architected multi-account
environment. You can build one by hand.
<br />· <strong>AWS Control Tower</strong> — the <em>service that builds and maintains</em> a
landing zone for you, using Organizations underneath.
<br />If a question asks for the fastest way to establish a governed multi-account environment,
the answer is <strong>Control Tower</strong>. If it asks what enforces a permission ceiling, the
answer is an <strong>SCP</strong>.
</Callout>

The last one is the argument that wins the meeting, and it is not the obvious one. Guardrails
are usually sold as restriction, but their real effect is **permission to stop supervising**.
Once an SCP makes it impossible to launch in an unapproved Region, spend beyond a boundary, or
disable the audit trail, a team can be handed an account and left alone — because the damage
they can do has a ceiling somebody already agreed to.

## How people actually log in

The **IAM Identity Center** half is the piece that makes a multi-account estate usable rather
than infuriating. Without it, thirty accounts means thirty sets of credentials and a spreadsheet.

With it, an engineer signs in once against the corporate directory and picks an account and a
role. On the command line the same thing works through an SSO profile:

```bash
aws configure sso          # once, per profile
aws sso login --profile production-readonly
aws s3 ls --profile production-readonly
```

The credentials that arrive are **temporary**, they expire, and there is no access key stored
anywhere. This is the *How IAM Decides* rule applied to human beings rather than applications:
nobody holds a long-lived credential, so nothing long-lived can leak.

<Callout type="cost">
Organizations, Control Tower, SCPs and IAM Identity Center have <strong>no direct charge</strong>
— you pay for what the underlying services do, chiefly CloudTrail and Config in the log archive
account, which on a large estate is not nothing. Consolidated billing usually pays for that by
itself, since usage across all accounts pools into the same volume discount tiers and Savings
Plans can be shared estate-wide.
</Callout>

It is worth ending this path where a real career tends to end up.

Projects 1 to 9 are about building things. This one is about the conditions under which other
people can build things without you watching — and at a certain scale that becomes the actual
job. Not designing the architecture, but designing **the space in which a hundred other people
design architectures**, and making the expensive mistakes structurally impossible rather than
merely discouraged.

That is what "Solutions Architect" tends to mean by the time anybody is paying well for it.
