## 09:00 — the change half the world ignored

Meridian moved their marketing site to a new host on a Tuesday morning. The job took eleven
minutes: copy the files across, point the domain at the new address, refresh. Two of the four of
them saw the new site straight away. They told their biggest customer it was live and went to
lunch.

At 15:00 the customer replied with a screenshot of the old site.

They changed the record again. Same result — new site on their laptops, old site in the
customer's office. Somebody suggested the change had not saved. Somebody else said, not entirely
as a joke, that the internet was broken.

It was not broken. It was doing precisely what it had been told, months earlier, by them. The
old record carried a **TTL of 86400** — a number nobody had chosen or looked at, sitting in a
field nobody had opened. Eighty-six thousand four hundred seconds is twenty-four hours. They had
asked every machine on the internet to remember the old address for a full day, and the internet
had politely obliged.

> Nothing on the internet asks *"where is meridian.io?"* and waits for the true answer. It asks
> once, remembers the reply for exactly as long as it was told to, and serves that memory to
> everyone who asks in the meantime.

That single sentence explains more support tickets than any other fact in this lesson. But to
see why it has to work that way, you have to follow the whole journey — because the address
lookup is only the first of two completely separate jobs.

## Two jobs, not one

Pressing Enter starts two things that have nothing to do with each other, and almost every
confusion about the internet comes from treating them as one:

1. **Find the machine.** Turn a name a human can remember into a number a network can route to.
   This is DNS, and it happens before your browser has spoken to the website at all.
2. **Talk to the machine.** Open a connection, agree how to encrypt it, and only then ask for
   the page. This is TCP, TLS and HTTP, in that order, every time.

Job one can succeed while job two fails, and vice versa. "The site is down" is nearly always one
or the other, and knowing which halves the time it takes to find out.

## The address itself

Before anything travels anywhere, the browser takes the address apart. Every piece is an
instruction to a different piece of machinery:

```
https://www.example.com:443/pricing?plan=pro#faq
```

| Part | Name | Who reads it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `https` | **scheme** | The browser. Says which language to speak, and implies port 443 |
| `www` | **subdomain** | DNS. A label the domain's owner invented, pointing wherever they like |
| `example` | **domain** | The registry. The part actually registered and paid for |
| `.com` | **top-level domain** | The root servers. Decides which registry is asked |
| `:443` | **port** | The server's operating system. Almost always left out, because the scheme implies it |
| `/pricing` | **path** | **The web server** — the first part it ever sees |
| `?plan=pro` | **query string** | The application. Extra parameters, visible in logs |
| `#faq` | **fragment** | Nobody but your browser. It is **never sent to the server** |

Read that column on the right again. **Everything up to the port is used to find a machine.
Everything from the path onwards is the only part that machine ever hears.** The site has no
idea which subdomain you typed unless the browser tells it separately — which it does, in a
header, precisely because it would otherwise be lost.

## Finding the machine: DNS is delegation, not a directory

Here is the idea that makes DNS click, and it is not the one most people are given.

**There is no list.** No machine anywhere holds a directory of every domain on the internet. The
system works by *delegation* — each level knows only who to ask next:

| Asked | Knows | Answers with |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Root servers** | Which registry runs each ending | "The `.com` servers handle that" |
| **`.com` registry** | Which nameserver each domain appointed | "Ask `ns1.example.com`" |
| **Authoritative nameserver** | The actual records for that one domain | `93.184.216.34` |

Three questions, each narrowing the field, and only the last one returns an address. That is why
registering a domain is really the act of telling a registry *which nameservers to point people
at* — and why changing hosting means changing a record on those nameservers rather than telling
anybody's browser anything.

The **resolver** is the machine that walks that chain on your behalf. Your laptop never talks to
the root servers; it asks one resolver and waits. Because that resolver serves thousands of
people, the answer it caches is reused by all of them — which is the entire reason the internet
can survive its own popularity, and also the entire reason Meridian's afternoon went the way it
did.

<Callout type="gotcha" title="TTL — the number that decides how long you are wrong for">
Every DNS record carries a <strong>TTL</strong> (time to live) in seconds: how long anyone may
remember the answer before asking again. Set it to <code>86400</code> and you have asked the
world to ignore your next change for a day.
<br /><br />
<strong>The fix is always the same and it has to happen in advance:</strong> lower the TTL to
something like 300 (five minutes) at least a full old-TTL before you move anything. Then make
the change, watch it take effect in minutes, and put the TTL back up. You cannot lower it
retroactively — the old value is already out there being obeyed.
</Callout>

<Callout type="exam">
An <strong>A record</strong> maps a name to an IPv4 address; <strong>AAAA</strong> does the same
for IPv6; a <strong>CNAME</strong> points one name at another name. On AWS, Route 53 adds an
<strong>alias record</strong>, which behaves like a CNAME but can sit at the root of your domain
and costs nothing to query — which is why exam answers about pointing <code>example.com</code>
itself at a load balancer or CloudFront are almost always "alias record".
</Callout>

## Talking to the machine: three handshakes, in a fixed order

Your browser now has an address. It still cannot ask for the page, because three conversations
have to finish first. They always happen in this order, and each one exists for a different
reason.

**1. TCP — "are you there, and are you listening?"** Three short messages: your machine sends
`SYN`, the server replies `SYN-ACK`, your machine sends `ACK`. Nothing useful has been exchanged
— this is purely both ends agreeing that a connection exists. **TCP** is also the part that
numbers your packets, notices when one goes missing, and asks for it again, which is why you can
download a file over a flaky connection and still get an intact file.

**2. TLS — "prove you are who you claim, and let us agree a secret."** The server presents a
**certificate**. Your browser checks two things: that it was signed by a **certificate
authority** it already trusts, and that it names the domain you actually typed. Then both sides
agree a one-off session key, and everything after this point is unreadable to anyone in between.
This is the padlock, and it is the `S` in HTTPS.

**3. HTTP — the actual request.** Only now does the browser say what it wants:

```
GET /pricing HTTP/2
Host: www.example.com
Accept: text/html
```

And the server answers, starting with its three-digit verdict:

```
HTTP/2 200 OK
Content-Type: text/html
Content-Length: 41822
```

<Callout type="gotcha" title="The padlock does not mean the site is safe">
It means exactly two things: the connection is encrypted, and somebody verified that this server
controls this domain name. It says nothing about whether the people running the site are honest.
A criminal can get a perfectly valid certificate for their own domain in about ninety seconds,
and most phishing sites now have one.
</Callout>

## The road itself: packets, routers and hops

Your request does not travel as one piece, and it does not travel along a route anybody planned.

It is cut into **packets** — small chunks, each wrapped in labels saying where it came from and
where it is going. Each packet is handed to a **router**, a machine whose entire job is to read
the destination, decide which neighbour is closer to it, and pass it on. That is one **hop**.
Ten to thirty hops later the packets arrive, possibly by different paths and out of order, and
TCP puts them back in sequence.

**No machine on that path knows the whole route.** Each router knows only its neighbours and,
via **BGP**, which addresses those neighbours claim they can reach. The route emerges from a few
thousand local decisions, which is why the internet keeps working when a cable is cut.

You can watch it happen. `traceroute` (or `tracert` on Windows) prints every hop:

```
traceroute to 93.184.216.34
 1  192.168.1.1        1 ms     ← your home router
 2  isp-gateway        9 ms     ← your ISP
 3  lon-ix            11 ms     ← an internet exchange in London
 4  ae-12.core1       78 ms     ← across the Atlantic
 5  93.184.216.34     84 ms     ← the server
```

Two pieces of jargon fall out of that listing. Hop 1 is doing **NAT** — network address
translation — which is how every device in your house shares one public address. Hop 3 is an
**IXP**, an internet exchange point: a physical building where networks plug into each other,
which is a surprisingly literal answer to "where is the internet".

## Where the time actually goes

This is the part that matters for everything you will learn about AWS afterwards. A typical
first visit from the UK to a server in the United States:

| Stage | Roughly | What it is |
| --- | --- | --- |
| DNS lookup | 0–20 ms | Usually zero — somebody already asked |
| TCP handshake | ~80 ms | **One round trip** before a single byte of your request moves |
| TLS handshake | ~160 ms | **One or two more round trips** |
| Request and first byte back | ~80 ms | **One more round trip**, plus the server's own work |
| Everything else | ~400 ms | The CSS, JavaScript, fonts and images the HTML asked for |

Almost none of that is data moving. It is **round trips** — messages that must travel all the
way there and all the way back before the next one can begin. And a round trip is limited by
physics: light in glass covers roughly 200 km per millisecond, and London to Virginia and back
is about 11,000 km whatever you pay.

**Therefore the only real lever is distance.** You cannot make the round trip faster; you can
only make it shorter, by putting a copy of the thing nearer the person asking. That single
sentence is the reason AWS has Regions in dozens of countries, the reason CloudFront exists, and
the reason "put it closer to the user" is the correct answer to a startling number of exam
questions.

The habit worth taking from this: when something is slow across a distance, ask <strong>how many
round trips</strong> before you ask how fast the server is. Latency is a distance problem, and
distance problems are solved by moving a copy — which is exactly what the next lesson is about.
</Scenario>

Meridian's fix took thirty seconds and had to happen a day early: drop the TTL to 300, wait out
the old twenty-four hours, then move. Their next migration went live everywhere within about
four minutes.

What actually changed was not their configuration. It was that somebody on the team could finally
say what the internet was *doing* — that no change is ever pushed to anybody, that every machine
in the chain is answering from memory on a schedule they themselves had published, and that the
customer's office had not been wrong or stale or broken. It had been correct, according to
instructions Meridian gave it in a field they had never opened.

Almost everything ahead of you on this site is AWS putting its own machinery into this journey:
Route 53 becomes the authoritative nameserver, CloudFront becomes a copy of your site sitting
closer to the person asking, a load balancer becomes the thing on the far end of that TCP
handshake. None of it replaces the journey. It just moves pieces of it nearer to whoever pressed
Enter.
