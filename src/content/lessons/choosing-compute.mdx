AWS has dozens of ways to run code, and they intimidate people because they look like dozens
of unrelated products. They are not. They are **positions on a single line**, and the line
measures one thing: how much of the machine you are still holding.

Every step along it takes work away from you and takes control away with it. That is the
entire trade, and every exam question about compute is a disguised question about where on
this line the scenario belongs.

## EC2 — the one that feels like a server, because it is one

**Amazon EC2** (Elastic Compute Cloud) gives you a virtual machine. You choose the operating
system, the size, the disk, and the network it sits in, and then it is yours: log in, install
things, and it keeps running until you stop it.

Instance types are named in a pattern worth being able to read, because questions quote them
without explanation. In `m5.large`:

- **`m`** is the *family* — what the machine is optimised for.
- **`5`** is the *generation*. Higher is newer, and usually cheaper per unit of work.
- **`large`** is the *size*. Each step up roughly doubles CPU and memory, and the price.

<Callout type="gotcha">
`t` instances are the trap. They are cheap because they assume you are mostly idle — they
accumulate **CPU credits** while quiet and spend them when busy. Run a `t3.micro` flat out
and it will exhaust its credits and be throttled to a fraction of a CPU, or silently charge
you for extra credits. Excellent for a dev box; wrong for a steady production load.
</Callout>

## Containers — the same app, packed more tightly

A container is your application plus everything it needs to run, sealed into an image that
behaves identically wherever it lands. That solves "it worked on my laptop", and it lets you
run six applications on one machine without them fighting over library versions.

AWS gives you two orchestrators — the thing that decides which container runs where, restarts
the ones that die, and replaces them during a deployment:

| | **Amazon ECS** | **Amazon EKS** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it is | AWS's own container orchestrator | Managed **Kubernetes** |
| Reach for it when | You want the simplest path and you are all-in on AWS | You already use Kubernetes, or need to stay portable across clouds |
| Cost of the control plane | Free | An hourly charge per cluster |
| Learning curve | Modest | Substantial |

Both can run their containers in one of two places, and **this is the distinction the exam
actually tests**:

- **On EC2 launch type** — the containers run on instances *you* own and can log into. You
  pick their size, you patch them, you pay for them whether or not a container is on them.
- **On Fargate** — you never see an instance. You declare "this task needs 0.5 vCPU and 1 GB"
  and AWS runs it somewhere. No patching, no capacity planning, a higher price per unit of
  compute, and no way to log into the host.

<Callout type="exam">
"The company wants to run containers but does not want to manage the underlying servers or
patch operating systems" → **Fargate**. It is one of the most reliably worded questions on
the SAA exam, and the words "manage the underlying infrastructure" are the tell.
</Callout>

## Lambda — code without a server in sight

With **AWS Lambda** you upload a function, connect it to something that triggers it — an HTTP
request through API Gateway, a file landing in S3, a message on a queue, a timer — and AWS
runs it on demand. You are billed for the number of invocations and the milliseconds each one
took, rounded to the nearest millisecond.

When nothing calls it, you pay **nothing at all**. Not a reduced rate — nothing. For workloads
that are quiet most of the day, this is not a small saving; it can be the difference between
£300 a month and 40p.

The constraints are what push workloads back off Lambda, and they are examinable:

- **15-minute maximum** per invocation. A four-hour video transcode cannot be one Lambda.
- **10 GB memory maximum**, and CPU is allocated in proportion to memory — you tune speed by
  buying memory.
- **Ephemeral**. Nothing you write to local disk survives reliably. State goes to S3, DynamoDB
  or somewhere else outside the function.
- **Cold starts.** The first invocation after a quiet period pays a start-up penalty, from
  milliseconds to a couple of seconds depending on runtime. *Provisioned Concurrency* keeps
  instances warm if that latency matters.

<Callout type="gotcha">
Serverless does not mean there is no server. It means <em>you</em> have no server: nothing to
choose, nothing to patch, nothing to pay for while idle, and scaling that happens without you
configuring it. Judge a service by those four properties, not by the marketing word.
</Callout>

<Scenario
  who="A company that processes insurance claim photographs"
  problem="Claims arrive in bursts — nothing overnight, then two thousand photos between 9am and 11am. Each photo needs resizing, an OCR pass, and a fraud-detection model that takes about 40 minutes to run. Today it all sits on four EC2 instances sized for the 11am peak, running all night doing nothing."
  solution="Resizing and OCR become Lambda functions triggered by the photo landing in S3 — seconds each, thousands in parallel, nothing running at 3am. The 40-minute fraud model becomes a Fargate task pulled from an SQS queue, started on demand and stopped when the queue empties."
  outcome="Overnight compute cost goes to zero, the 9am burst is absorbed by Lambda scaling out automatically, and the four permanently-on instances are decommissioned."

Note that this workload was split across **two** compute services rather than forced into
one. The 15-minute Lambda ceiling was the deciding fact for the fraud model — and recognising
that ceiling is exactly what the corresponding exam question is checking.
