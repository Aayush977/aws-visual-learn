## The sentence that was doing all the work

A payroll bureau pays 40,000 people on the last Thursday of every month. Their disaster-recovery
plan was one sentence, and everyone in the company had heard it: *we take a nightly backup and
copy it to another Region.*

It was true. The backups ran, they were copied, the job had not failed in two years.

Then, at somebody's insistence, they ran a tabletop exercise — no outage, no drama, just four
people in a room with a stopwatch walking through what they would actually do. Restoring the
database and bringing the application up in the second Region came to **about eleven hours**.
And because the backup was nightly, a failure on payroll Wednesday would lose a day of entered
timesheets, which meant thousands of people re-keying data they had already keyed.

Nobody had been careless. They had a real control, tested by nobody, described by a sentence
that quietly answered a different question than the one that mattered. "Do we have backups?"
was answered. "How long can we be down, and how much can we lose?" had never been asked out
loud, and so had never been costed, argued about or signed off.

The Well-Architected Framework exists to make somebody ask it — along with five other questions
of the same uncomfortable kind, before the day you need the answer.

## Six questions about a design

The **AWS Well-Architected Framework** is AWS's written-down answer to "what does good look
like?". It is a set of six pillars, each of which is really a question you ask about a design.

It matters for two reasons. On the exam, a scenario that stresses one pillar is usually telling
you which family of services to reach for. In real work, it is a checklist that catches the
thing everybody forgot — which is nearly always operations or cost.

## The design principles worth remembering

Across the pillars, a handful of principles come up again and again, and they are quoted almost
verbatim in exam answers:

- **Stop guessing capacity.** Scale automatically instead of predicting.
- **Test systems at production scale**, then throw the environment away — something you can only
  afford in the cloud.
- **Automate to make architectural experimentation easier.** Infrastructure as code means an
  experiment costs an afternoon, not a quarter.
- **Allow for evolutionary architectures.** The design should be able to change, because it will.
- **Drive architectures using data.** Measure, do not argue.
- **Improve through game days.** Deliberately break things in a controlled way and learn what
  happens.
- **Design for failure.** Assume every component will fail, because at scale everything does.

<Callout type="exam">
The <strong>AWS Well-Architected Tool</strong> is a free service in the console that walks you
through these questions for a workload and produces a list of risks. If a question asks how to
<em>review</em> an existing architecture against best practices, that is the answer — not
Trusted Advisor, which checks live resources rather than design decisions.
</Callout>

## Reliability, in numbers: RTO and RPO

Reliability questions get precise, and they get precise with two acronyms. Both are measured in
time, and confusing them is a standard trap.

- **RTO — Recovery Time Objective.** *How long can we be down?* You are measuring forward from
  the failure to the moment service resumes.
- **RPO — Recovery Point Objective.** *How much data can we afford to lose?* You are measuring
  backward from the failure to the last usable copy of the data.

A nightly backup gives you an RPO of up to 24 hours — everything since last night's backup is
gone. If restoring that backup takes three hours, your RTO is three hours. They are independent:
you can have an excellent RPO and a dreadful RTO, and often do.

The payroll bureau's real numbers were an **RTO of 11 hours** and an **RPO of up to 24**. What
the business could actually tolerate, once someone made them write it down, was one hour and
five minutes respectively. Those two pairs of numbers are the entire disaster-recovery
conversation: the moment both are on the same slide, the strategy chooses itself and the
argument about money takes one meeting instead of a year.

<Callout type="analogy">
<strong>RPO</strong> is “when did I last save my document?” — it decides how much work you lose.
<strong>RTO</strong> is “how long until I am typing again?” — it decides how long you are stuck.
Saving constantly does not help if the laptop takes a day to replace.
</Callout>

## The four disaster-recovery strategies

Ordered from cheapest and slowest to most expensive and fastest. The exam expects you to match
a described RTO/RPO to the right one.

| Strategy | What is running in the second Region | RTO | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Backup & Restore** | Nothing. Just backups and snapshots | Hours to days | Lowest |
| **Pilot Light** | The critical core only — typically a replicating database, servers switched off | Tens of minutes | Low |
| **Warm Standby** | A complete but **scaled-down** copy, running and serving nothing | Minutes | Medium |
| **Multi-Site Active/Active** | A full copy, taking real traffic | Near zero | Highest |

<Callout type="gotcha">
The distinction that gets missed: <strong>Pilot Light has the servers switched off</strong>,
with only the data replicating. <strong>Warm Standby is running</strong>, just small. If a
question says “a scaled-down version of the environment is always running”, that is Warm
Standby, not Pilot Light.
</Callout>

The payroll bureau now fails over in about six minutes and loses under a minute of data. It cost
them roughly 30% more, approved in a single meeting — because by then the choice was not
"spend more on resilience" but "spend this, or re-key 40,000 timesheets".

That is what the framework is for, and why it is six *questions* rather than six answers. Not
one of those six will tell you what to build. What they do is force somebody to say a number out
loud — how long down, how much lost, how much per month, who gets paged — while it is still
cheap to change the answer.

Every pillar is a question that is uncomfortable early and unaffordable late.
