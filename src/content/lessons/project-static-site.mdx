## The problem

Kettle & Fox — the Yorkshire homeware shop whose website went down on live television back in
the first lesson — need somewhere to put their brochure site. Product photography, an about
page, opening hours, a stockist map.

It is *read* by everybody and *written* by two people about once a month. There is no login, no
basket, no database. And on the one Tuesday a year when a television presenter mentions them,
twenty thousand people arrive in nine minutes.

Almost everybody's instinct is a web server. A small EC2 instance, nginx, the files on disk.
That works, and it drags in a surprising amount of luggage: an operating system to patch, a
disk that can fill, an instance that can fail, an Availability Zone that can fail, and a
capacity decision you have to make in advance and get wrong.

Here is the same site with none of that.

**Route 53** is here for one specific reason, and the quickest way to understand it is to try
the obvious thing and watch DNS stop you.

1. **You own `kettleandfox.co.uk` and you want CloudFront to serve it.** So you go looking for
   CloudFront's address — and CloudFront does not give you one. It gives you a **name**:

   ```
   d111abcdef8.cloudfront.net
   ```

   There is no fixed number to hand you, because the site is answered from hundreds of edge
   locations whose addresses change constantly.

2. **Pointing a name at another name is a `CNAME` record.** So that is what you try to add:

   ```
   kettleandfox.co.uk.   CNAME   d111abcdef8.cloudfront.net
   ```

3. **It is refused.** And not by AWS — by any DNS provider you attempt it with.

**Why.** The bare name, with nothing in front of it, is called the **apex** of the domain. The
apex already has to hold the records that say who runs the domain: `NS`, naming the
authoritative name servers, and `SOA`. And a `CNAME` is not allowed to share a name with any
other record — because a `CNAME` means *"everything about this name is answered elsewhere"*,
which cannot be true of a name that is also holding its own `NS` records.

The apex must have those records. So the apex can never be a `CNAME`. It is in the DNS
specification, which is why every provider refuses it and why no amount of looking for the
right setting will help.

**The fix is Route 53's `ALIAS` record.** It answers like an `A` record — a name to a number,
which *is* legal at the apex — but you configure it by pointing at an AWS resource, and Route 53
looks up CloudFront's current addresses at the moment of the query. Legal where the rules are
looking, flexible where you need it.

<Callout type="exam">
If a question says the site must work at <code>example.com</code> <em>as well as</em>
<code>www.example.com</code>, and the target is CloudFront or a load balancer, the answer is a
<strong>Route 53 alias record</strong>. Options offering a CNAME for the root domain are wrong,
and one is offered every time.
</Callout>

The record types, TTLs and the caching behaviour behind all of this are covered properly in
<a href={lessonUrl("how-the-edge-works")}>How the Edge Works</a>.

**CloudFront** is doing two jobs at once, and beginners usually notice only the first.
*Latency*: a visitor in Sydney gets the page from Sydney. *Origin protection*: the bucket
itself never faces the public, requests are absorbed at the edge, and — the part nobody
mentions — **data transfer out via CloudFront is cheaper than straight from S3**, so the CDN
partly pays for itself.

<Callout type="gotcha" title="The bucket should not be public here">
The <em>First 30 Minutes</em> lesson had you make a bucket public on purpose, which is fine for
learning. In production you do the opposite: the bucket stays private and CloudFront reaches it
through <strong>Origin Access Control</strong>, an identity CloudFront uses that the bucket
policy trusts.

Why bother, when the content is public anyway? Because it forces every request through
CloudFront, where your caching, your logging and your WAF rules live. A public bucket can be hit
directly, bypassing all three — and running up S3 bandwidth charges you never see coming.
</Callout>

## Roughly what it costs

Order-of-magnitude figures for a small brochure site, London pricing. Check the
**Pricing Calculator** for anything you actually intend to build.

| Line | Rough monthly cost |
| --- | --- |
| S3 storage — a few GB of images | pennies |
| CloudFront data transfer — modest traffic | a few pounds |
| Route 53 hosted zone | about $0.50, fixed |
| ACM certificate | free |
| **Typical total** | **a few pounds a month** |

The interesting part is not the total, it is the **shape**. There is no fixed cost for capacity
sitting idle — the television Tuesday costs a little more than a quiet Tuesday, and a quiet
Tuesday costs almost nothing. Compare that with the EC2 version, which costs the same every
single day whether anybody visits or not.

Worth pausing on what is absent here, because it is the whole point.

No operating system. No patching. No instance type chosen months in advance. No Auto Scaling
group, no load balancer, no health checks — and no Availability Zone that can take the site with
it, because there is no zone involved at all.

Kettle & Fox's television Tuesday would not have registered as an event. **That is what
"serverless" actually buys**, and it is why this unglamorous little architecture is the one to
understand before any of the others.
