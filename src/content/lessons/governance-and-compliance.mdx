## Three questions, no answers

Aldgate Health's first SOC 2 audit lasted about four minutes before it stopped being
comfortable. The auditor asked three questions, in a mild tone, one after another.

*Who changed the production database's security group last March?*

*Has any S3 bucket in this account ever been public?*

*Show me the certification covering the physical data centre this runs in.*

Nobody in the room thought these were unfair. Every one of them was, in principle, a knowable
fact about a system they owned. And they could not answer a single one.

The first was gone — CloudTrail's default free view holds 90 days of management events, and
March was long past. The second had never been recorded at all, because nothing was tracking
what resources *looked like* over time, only what people did. The third they could have had in
sixty seconds from a service they did not know existed, and instead they emailed their account
manager and waited nine days.

The team's security was genuinely fine. They had simply never separated two things that sound
identical: **being secure, and being able to demonstrate it afterwards.**

## Demonstrating, not securing

Security services stop bad things happening. **Governance** is the separate question of whether
you can *demonstrate* that your account looks the way you claim it does — to a regulator, an
auditor, or your own security team six months after everyone forgot what they configured.

The Cloud Practitioner exam leans on this more heavily than people expect, and it does it with
four services that are easy to blur together.

## CloudTrail versus Config — the distinction everything else rests on

Both record things. They record **different** things, and swapping them is the single most
common wrong answer in this area.

| | **AWS CloudTrail** | **AWS Config** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Records | **Events** — API calls | **State** — resource configuration over time |
| Answers | "*Who* deleted the bucket, and when?" | "*What did* the bucket's settings look like last Tuesday?" |
| Also does | Delivers logs to S3 and CloudWatch Logs | Evaluates **rules** and flags non-compliance |
| Default | Last 90 days of management events, free | Off; charged per configuration item recorded |

A useful sentence: **CloudTrail is CCTV of the door; Config is a photograph of the room, taken
every time anything moves.**

<Callout type="exam">
Watch for the verb. "<strong>Who</strong> made this change?" and "audit trail of API activity"
are <strong>CloudTrail</strong>. "<strong>Which</strong> resources are non-compliant?", "alert
when a security group allows 0.0.0.0/0", and "configuration history" are <strong>Config</strong>.
</Callout>

## Organizations and Control Tower

**AWS Organizations** groups many AWS accounts, gives you one bill, and — the governance part —
lets you attach **Service Control Policies** that cap what a member account may ever do. This
is *preventive*: an action outside the boundary fails, even for that account's root user.

Separate accounts are the strongest isolation boundary AWS offers, so the recommended pattern
is many accounts (production, development, security, logging) inside one organisation,
organised into **organizational units** with SCPs attached at the OU level.

**AWS Control Tower** does that setup for you. It creates a **landing zone** — Organizations,
a log-archive account, an audit account, CloudTrail, Config — plus a catalogue of
**guardrails** you switch on:

- **Preventive** guardrails are implemented as SCPs. They stop the action.
- **Detective** guardrails are implemented as Config rules. They report the drift.

Its **Account Factory** provisions new accounts that already comply on day one.

<Callout type="gotcha">
"Guardrail" is a Control Tower word covering both mechanisms, and the exam sometimes tests which
is which. <strong>Preventive = SCP = it cannot happen. Detective = Config rule = it happened and
here is the finding.</strong>
</Callout>

## Artifact and Audit Manager — two directions of compliance

These sound identical and point in opposite directions.

- **AWS Artifact** is a **download portal**. It gives you AWS's own audit reports — SOC 1/2/3,
  PCI DSS, ISO 27001, FedRAMP — and lets you accept agreements such as the BAA for HIPAA. This
  is evidence about **AWS's** side of the shared responsibility model, and it is what you hand
  an auditor who asks whether your provider is certified.
- **AWS Audit Manager** continuously collects evidence from **your** account and maps it to a
  framework (PCI DSS, GDPR, SOC 2, HIPAA), producing an assessment report. This is evidence
  about **your** side.

## The compliance vocabulary the exam uses

You are not expected to know these standards, only to recognise that AWS holds them and where
to get the paperwork:

- **SOC 1 / 2 / 3** — controls at a service organisation. Available in Artifact.
- **PCI DSS** — handling card data. AWS is a certified Level 1 Service Provider; you are still
  responsible for your own application.
- **ISO 27001** — information security management.
- **HIPAA** — US health data. AWS supports it under a Business Associate Addendum accepted in
  Artifact.
- **GDPR** — EU personal data. AWS provides a Data Processing Addendum; where the data lives is
  your choice, which is why **Region selection** is a compliance decision.

<Callout type="exam">
Under the Shared Responsibility Model, <strong>AWS's certifications never make your application
compliant.</strong> A question describing a company that "must be PCI compliant" is asking about
what <em>they</em> must configure — Artifact only proves AWS's half.
</Callout>

---

Map the auditor's three questions onto the four services and the whole topic arranges itself:

| The question | The service | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *Who changed it?* | **CloudTrail** | A log of API calls, with a name and a timestamp |
| *What did it look like, and when?* | **Config** | A timeline of resource state, plus rules that flag drift |
| *Is AWS's half certified?* | **Artifact** | AWS's own SOC 2, ISO and PCI reports, downloadable |
| *Is our half certified?* | **Audit Manager** | Continuously gathers evidence against a framework |

Aldgate passed on the second attempt, and the change that mattered was not a new control. It was
an SCP that makes it impossible for anyone in the organisation to stop CloudTrail — because the
lesson of the first audit was never that somebody had done something wrong.

It was that **evidence has to be collected before you know you need it**, by something that
cannot be switched off by whoever most wants it switched off.
