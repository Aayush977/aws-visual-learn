## The problem

Meridian's customers send them data in whatever shape it arrives: nightly CSV exports, JSON
from an API, the occasional spreadsheet somebody emails. It adds up to a few terabytes and grows
every month.

The traditional answer is a data warehouse: design a schema, build a pipeline that transforms
everything into it, and load it in. That works, and it has a cost people underestimate — **you
must decide what the data means before you store it**, and every time a customer adds a column
or a new question gets asked, the schema and the pipeline both have to change.

Meanwhile a warehouse cluster runs, and bills, whether anybody queries it or not. For a
four-person company where somebody asks a serious analytical question perhaps twice a week,
that is a large amount of money spent on readiness.

A data lake inverts the order. **Store first, decide what it means when you ask.**

**Glue** is the piece newcomers skip and then cannot work out why nothing queries. Athena does
not read S3 blindly — it needs to know that a folder of files is a *table* with *columns*, and
that is precisely what the **Glue Data Catalog** holds. The crawler is what fills it in
automatically. Glue also does the ETL work when raw files genuinely do need transforming, but
its catalogue role is what makes the lake queryable at all.

**S3 rather than a database** for storage comes down to three properties this pattern depends
on: it is cheap enough that keeping everything is reasonable, it has **eleven nines** of
durability so the lake is also the archive, and lifecycle rules can move old partitions to
Glacier classes without changing a single query.

<Callout type="cost" title="Athena bills per terabyte scanned — so scan less">
This is where data lakes get expensive, and it is entirely avoidable. Athena charges roughly
<strong>$5 per terabyte scanned</strong>, and <code>SELECT *</code> across an unpartitioned
bucket scans everything, every time.

Two fixes, both worth remembering: <strong>partition</strong> the data (typically by date, so a
query for last month reads one month of files rather than four years of them), and store it in a
<strong>columnar format like Parquet</strong>, so a query touching three columns reads three
columns instead of all forty. Together these routinely cut the bill by an order of magnitude
without changing a single query.
</Callout>

## Roughly what it costs

| Line | Rough monthly cost |
| --- | --- |
| S3 storage — a few TB, with lifecycle rules | tens of pounds |
| Glue crawler — a short run daily | a few pounds |
| Athena — per TB scanned, partitioned sensibly | a few pounds at this volume |
| QuickSight — per author/reader | roughly $9–24 per user |
| **Typical total for a small team** | **tens of pounds, dominated by storage** |

The shape is worth noticing again: with a Redshift cluster the bill starts at hundreds of pounds
and stays there whether anybody logs in. Here, a quiet month genuinely costs almost nothing —
and a month where somebody runs a badly-written query over unpartitioned data can cost more than
the cluster would have. **The cost moves from readiness to usage, which cuts both ways.**

The quiet radicalism of this architecture is the decision it lets you postpone.

Every warehouse design forces you to say, up front and permanently, what your data means. A lake
lets you keep the raw thing and answer next year's question — the one nobody has thought of yet
— without having thrown away the column that turns out to matter.

That is worth real money in a way that is hard to put on a slide, and it is why "keep everything
in S3, work it out later" became the default posture for analytics on AWS.
