## The finding nobody read

A retailer failed a penetration test three ways in one afternoon. A public bucket full of
exported orders, including card details. Two instances running a web framework with a critical,
published, months-old vulnerability. And an access key being used from a country the company has
never traded in.

The third one is the interesting one, because **AWS had already spotted it.**

GuardDuty was switched on. It had raised a finding — the key, the country, the hour, all of it —
eleven weeks before the pen-testers arrived. The finding sat in a console that nobody had a
reason to open, next to a hundred other findings, because there was no route from that console
to a human being.

The other two were worse in a quieter way: nothing was watching for them at all. No service was
looking inside the buckets, and no service was checking what was installed on the instances.

Three findings, three completely different failures, and the lesson is not "buy more security
products". It is that these services are **not interchangeable and not optional extras of each
other** — each one answers exactly one question, and a question nobody asked has no answer
sitting anywhere.

## Six services, six questions

This is the group of services beginners find most interchangeable, because the names all sound
like security and the marketing all sounds the same. They are not interchangeable at all. Each
one answers exactly one question, and exam items are built by asking that question in plain
English and seeing whether you recognise it.

Split them by **where they sit**: some stand at the edge blocking traffic before it arrives,
and the rest sit inside watching what is already happening.

## The edge: Shield and WAF

**AWS Shield Standard** protects every AWS account automatically, at no cost, against the
common volumetric and network-layer DDoS attacks. You do not enable it and you cannot turn it
off. **Shield Advanced** costs about $3,000 a month and adds 24/7 access to the Shield Response
Team, detailed attack diagnostics, and — the reason people actually buy it — **cost protection**,
refunding the scaling charges an attack causes.

**AWS WAF** works one layer up, reading the HTTP request. You write rules, or subscribe to AWS
managed rule groups, to block:

- SQL injection and cross-site scripting patterns
- Requests from particular countries or IP ranges
- Too many requests from one source (**rate-based rules**)
- Anything matching a string, regex or size constraint you specify

It attaches to **CloudFront, an Application Load Balancer, API Gateway or AppSync** — never
directly to an EC2 instance.

<Callout type="exam">
The distinction is the layer. <strong>Shield = volume</strong>, an attack that tries to exhaust
your bandwidth or connections. <strong>WAF = content</strong>, a request that is individually
malicious. "Protect against SQL injection" is always WAF. "Absorb a large-scale DDoS attack" is
always Shield.
</Callout>

## The inside: GuardDuty, Inspector, Macie

These three all "detect" and are constantly swapped in wrong answers. Separate them by
**what they look at**:

| Service | Looks at | Finds |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **GuardDuty** | Logs — CloudTrail, VPC Flow Logs, DNS, EKS audit | *Behaviour* that is out of character: crypto-mining, compromised credentials, reconnaissance |
| **Inspector** | EC2, ECR images, Lambda | *Software* with known CVEs, and unintended network paths |
| **Macie** | S3 objects | *Data* that is sensitive: PII, financial identifiers, credentials |

All three are agentless or near-agentless, enabled with a click, and priced by volume analysed.

<Callout type="gotcha">
<strong>Security Hub detects nothing on its own.</strong> It aggregates and scores findings from
other services against standards such as CIS Foundations and AWS Foundational Security Best
Practices. If a question asks what actually <em>found</em> the problem, the answer is GuardDuty,
Inspector or Macie — Security Hub is where you then see it.
</Callout>

## The rest of the toolkit

Enough to recognise by name:

- **AWS Network Firewall** — stateful filtering for a whole VPC, including intrusion prevention.
  Beyond what security groups and NACLs can express.
- **AWS Firewall Manager** — centrally applies WAF rules, Shield Advanced protections and
  security group policies across **every account in an Organization**. The answer whenever a
  question says "consistently across all accounts".
- **Amazon Detective** — takes a GuardDuty finding and builds the investigation graph so you can
  work out how far an intrusion spread. Detection versus *investigation*.
- **AWS Security Hub** — as above, the aggregator.
- **Amazon Cognito** — user sign-up and sign-in for your *applications'* end users, with social
  and enterprise identity federation. Not for AWS console access; that is IAM Identity Center.
- **AWS Directory Service** — managed Microsoft Active Directory, for joining EC2 Windows
  instances to a domain or federating existing corporate identities.

<Callout type="exam">
Two identity services that get swapped: <strong>Cognito is for your application's users</strong>
(the customers of your mobile app), and <strong>IAM Identity Center is for your employees</strong>
(console and CLI access to AWS accounts). If the people in the question are customers, it is
Cognito.
</Callout>

The retailer's three findings each map to exactly one service — Macie for the bucket, Inspector
for the vulnerable instances, GuardDuty for the stolen key — and knowing which is which is what
the exam is testing.

But the eleven weeks are the part worth carrying into a job. GuardDuty did its entire job
perfectly and it changed nothing, because **a finding that reaches no human is indistinguishable
from a service you never enabled.** That is what Security Hub and an EventBridge rule into the
on-call channel are actually for: not detection, which was already working, but the short walk
from *detected* to *somebody knows*.

Detection you do not route is a very expensive way of being able to say, afterwards, that you
had a tool for that.
