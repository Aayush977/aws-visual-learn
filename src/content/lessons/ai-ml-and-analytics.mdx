## Forty calls out of twelve thousand

A call centre recorded every support call — twelve thousand a month — and kept them, dutifully,
for three years.

Their entire quality process was one supervisor listening to about forty of them. Three tenths
of one per cent. When the board asked which products were generating angry calls, the honest
answer was that nobody knew, and could not find out, because the evidence existed only as
eleven thousand nine hundred and sixty hours of audio nobody would ever play. A growing
Spanish-speaking segment was reviewed by no one at all.

None of that required machine learning to fix, in the sense of anybody building a model. It
required four services, wired together in an afternoon: one that turns audio into text, one
that translates, one that scores sentiment and picks out product names, and one that lets a
manager query the result.

The biggest single driver of angry calls turned out to be a delivery partner, which the company
had never suspected and had been paying for two years.

That is what this category is for, and it is why the exam tests it the way it does. **You are
never asked how any of these work.** You are asked to recognise a sentence — "convert calls to
text", "detect objects in images" — and name the one service that does that.

## Learn them as sentences, not as technology

This is the part of the blueprint people skip, and it is close to free marks.

## The AI services — one job each

Almost all of these are **pre-trained**: you call an API, you do not build a model.

## The analytics services — moving from raw data to an answer

Analytics questions describe a pipeline, and each stage has an obvious service.

| Stage | Service | The sentence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Ingest streams** | **Amazon Kinesis** | "Real-time streaming data" — clickstreams, IoT telemetry, logs as they happen |
| **Store** | **Amazon S3** | The data lake. Everything lands here |
| **Catalogue & transform** | **AWS Glue** | Serverless **ETL**, plus the Data Catalog that records what your files contain |
| **Query in place** | **Amazon Athena** | Serverless **SQL directly on S3**, billed per TB scanned |
| **Warehouse** | **Amazon Redshift** | Petabyte-scale analytics when many analysts query all day |
| **Big data frameworks** | **Amazon EMR** | Managed **Hadoop, Spark, Hive** for teams who already work that way |
| **Search & log analytics** | **Amazon OpenSearch Service** | Full-text search, log analytics, operational dashboards |
| **Visualise** | **Amazon QuickSight** | The BI dashboards that business users actually look at |

<Callout type="cost">
<strong>Athena vs Redshift</strong> is a frequency question, not a size question. Occasional
queries over data already in S3 → <strong>Athena</strong>, because you pay only for the scans.
Continuous querying by a team of analysts → <strong>Redshift</strong>, because dedicated compute
stops being the expensive option.
</Callout>

<Callout type="gotcha">
<strong>Kinesis is for data in motion; S3 and Redshift are for data at rest.</strong> If a
question says "real time", "streaming" or "as events arrive", it is Kinesis — even when the data
eventually lands in S3 anyway.
</Callout>

Nobody at that call centre trained a model, labelled a dataset or hired a data scientist. They
pointed four pre-trained services at audio files they already had, and the answer they had been
guessing at for two years fell out in a week.

Keep that shape in mind when you read an exam question here, because the wrong answer is almost
always **SageMaker**. SageMaker is for building a model that does not exist yet — genuinely
necessary, genuinely expensive in people and time. If the task is transcription, translation,
sentiment, object detection or text extraction, somebody has already built that model and rented
it to you by the API call.

The skill being tested is recognising when the problem is ordinary.
