## The bug that cost more than the team

Meridian — the four-person analytics company from the IAM lesson — had a nightly job that
pulled a 90 GB dataset out of S3 to a machine in their office, crunched it, and wrote a report.

Someone added a retry. Sensibly, they thought: the download occasionally failed, so wrap it and
try again. What they did not do was cap the attempts, and what they did not know was that the
job's disk had filled up, so every attempt failed at 99% and started over.

The loop ran for eleven days.

It downloaded that file just over four thousand times. **Putting the data into S3 had been
free; taking each copy back out cost roughly $8**, at somewhere near nine cents a gigabyte —
and nine cents a gigabyte is the kind of number that looks like a rounding error right up until
you multiply it by four thousand.

The invoice was about $32,000. The storage line on that same invoice, for the actual data, was
under $3.

Nobody had done anything exotic. They had simply never internalised the single most expensive
sentence in cloud computing:

> **Data going in is free. Data coming out is not.**

## The three things on every bill

AWS publishes hundreds of price lists, which makes the pricing model look impossibly complex.
It is not. Almost every line on almost every bill is one of three things:

1. **Compute** — how long something ran, and how big it was.
2. **Storage** — how many gigabytes you kept, and for how long.
3. **Data transfer** — specifically, data going **out**.

The third is the one that catches people, because it is invisible until it is not.

## Data transfer: the rule that saves the most money

Learn this shape, because it is directly examinable and it explains an enormous number of
architectural decisions:

- **Data IN from the internet — free.** Always. Upload as much as you like. *This is the half
  that lulls you: eleven days of uploading nothing and downloading everything looked, from the
  inside, like a job that was working.*
- **Data OUT to the internet — charged per GB**, and it is the single most common surprise on a
  bill. There is a modest free allowance each month.
- **Between Availability Zones in the same Region — charged**, in both directions. This is why
  chatty cross-AZ traffic quietly costs money.
- **Within the same Availability Zone using private IP addresses — free.**
- **Between Regions — charged**, at a higher rate than cross-AZ.
- **Out to CloudFront, and from CloudFront to users — cheaper** than straight from the origin,
  which is one reason a CDN often pays for itself.

<Callout type="cost">
Two specific tricks the exam likes. A <strong>gateway VPC endpoint for S3 or DynamoDB</strong>
is free and keeps that traffic off your NAT gateway, removing both the NAT data-processing
charge and the internet path. And putting <strong>CloudFront</strong> in front of an origin cuts
egress cost as well as latency — a rare case where the fast answer is also the cheap one.
</Callout>

## The Free Tier, which changed in 2025

This is worth getting right, because most study material online still describes the old
arrangement — and so, for now, do some exam questions.

**What a new account gets today.** Accounts start on a **Free plan**: **$100 in credits
immediately**, up to **$100 more** for completing getting-started activities, so **up to $200
over six months**. The plan ends after six months or when the credits are exhausted, whichever
comes first, and you are not charged unless you choose to move to a Paid plan. Alongside it,
**over 30 services are always free** within monthly usage limits — permanently, on either plan.
That always-free allowance includes things like 1 million Lambda requests a month and 25 GB of
DynamoDB storage.

**What the older material describes.** Accounts opened before the change used a three-part
structure, and you should still recognise it:

- **Always free** — permanent, for every account. Still true today.
- **12 months free** — from the day the account was opened. 750 hours a month of a `t2.micro`
  or `t3.micro`, 5 GB of S3 Standard, 750 hours of RDS.
- **Trials** — short-term, starting when you activate the service.

<Callout type="exam" title="Which one do I answer with?">
If a question describes the <strong>12-month free tier</strong> or asks you to distinguish the
three shapes, answer inside that framing — the exam guide has not been rewritten as quickly as
the offer changed. What does not change, and is what the question is really testing, is the
principle in the callout below: <strong>the free tier is an allowance, not a cap</strong>.
Verify current terms at <a href="https://aws.amazon.com/free/">the AWS Free Tier page</a> before
opening an account.
</Callout>

<Callout type="gotcha">
750 hours per month is <strong>one</strong> instance running continuously — not two. Run two
<code>t3.micro</code> instances all month and roughly half of that usage is billed. The free
tier also does not stop you spending: there is no cap, which is why setting up a
<strong>budget alert on day one</strong> is genuinely good advice.
</Callout>

<Callout type="exam">
Budgets versus Cost Explorer is a favourite pairing. <strong>Budgets</strong> is proactive — it
notifies you when a threshold is crossed. <strong>Cost Explorer</strong> is retrospective — it
shows you charts of what already happened. If the question contains "notify", "alert" or
"before it exceeds", the answer is Budgets.
</Callout>

## Organizations and consolidated billing

**AWS Organizations** lets you run many AWS accounts under one roof — which is the recommended
structure, because separate accounts are the strongest isolation boundary AWS offers. Put
production in one account and development in another and a mistake in one cannot touch the other.

What it does for money:

- **One bill** for every account.
- **Volume discounts aggregate.** Tiered pricing (S3 storage, data transfer) is calculated on
  the *combined* usage, so the whole organisation reaches cheaper tiers sooner.
- **Reserved Instances and Savings Plans are shared.** An unused reservation in one account
  automatically applies to matching usage in another.

And for control, **Service Control Policies** set the maximum permissions any member account can
have — the guardrail covered in the IAM lesson.

<Callout type="cost">
<strong>Cost allocation tags</strong> are how a single bill becomes useful. Tag every resource
with something like <code>Project</code>, <code>Environment</code> and <code>Owner</code>,
activate those tags in the billing console, and Cost Explorer can then break spend down by them.
Untagged resources are where unexplained cost hides, and a tagging policy applied early is worth
far more than one applied later.
</Callout>

## Where the savings usually are

For the exam, and for real life, in rough order of how much they typically return:

1. **Turn off what nobody uses.** Dev and test environments outside working hours; forgotten
   instances; unattached EBS volumes and Elastic IPs still being billed.
2. **Right-size.** Compute Optimizer routinely finds instances at 5% CPU.
3. **Commit** — Savings Plans on the steady baseline, once it is genuinely steady.
4. **Move interruptible work to Spot.**
5. **Apply S3 lifecycle rules** so cold data drifts into cheaper storage classes automatically.
6. **Cut data-transfer cost** with CloudFront and VPC endpoints.
7. **Go serverless** where the workload is spiky, so the idle hours cost nothing at all.

Meridian's $32,000 was refunded, in the end — AWS is often reasonable about a first, obvious,
self-reported runaway, though nobody should design around that.

The interesting part of the story is the eleven days. Not the bug: the bug was ordinary and
someone writes it every week. What made it expensive was that **nothing was watching**, and the
only mechanism in place for noticing was an invoice that arrives once a month, weeks after the
money is gone.

A budget alert takes four minutes to set up and would have caught this on day one. That is the
entire lesson: on AWS, the meter is always running, and the meter does not phone you unless you
ask it to.
