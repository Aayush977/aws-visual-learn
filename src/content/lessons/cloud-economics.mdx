## Two spreadsheets, one data centre

When Brightwell were deciding whether to close their data centre, two people built a cost model
and presented them in the same meeting.

The infrastructure manager's model said moving to AWS would cost about **40% more**. He had
taken the servers in the racks, priced equivalent EC2 instances, added storage, and compared
that to what the data centre cost the business each year. It was careful work and the
arithmetic was correct.

The finance director's model said it would **save about a third**. Also careful, also correct.

They had not disagreed about a single number. What they disagreed about was **which numbers
belonged in the comparison** — and the infrastructure manager's data-centre column contained
only the things that arrive as invoices. It had the hardware, the maintenance contracts and the
building. It did not have the electricity, because that was on the facilities budget. It did not
have the four days a month two engineers spent patching and replacing disks, because that was
salary and salary is paid anyway. It did not have the 60% of capacity sitting idle to cover a
peak that happened eleven times a year. It did not have the £600,000 hardware refresh due in
2028.

None of that was dishonesty. It is simply that **owning infrastructure hides most of its cost
in budgets nobody adds together**, and the cloud makes every one of those costs arrive as a
line on a single monthly bill — which feels more expensive precisely because you can finally
see it.

That is what cloud economics is about, and the exam has a whole task statement on it.

## Fixed cost and variable cost

Start with the distinction everything else hangs off.

- A **fixed cost** is paid before you know whether you needed it. A server bought in March
  costs the same whether it runs flat out or sits idle all year. In accounting terms this is
  **capital expenditure (CapEx)** — money spent up front on an asset you own and then
  depreciate over several years.
- A **variable cost** is paid because something happened. An instance that ran for nine hours
  costs nine hours. This is **operational expenditure (OpEx)** — money spent on what you
  consumed this month.

The cloud's central trade is turning the first into the second. That is genuinely valuable, but
notice what it is *not*: it is not automatically a discount. **It is the removal of the
requirement to guess.** You stop paying for capacity you might need and start paying for
capacity you did need.

<Callout type="exam">
Exam questions signal this with very consistent wording. <strong>"Avoid large up-front
investment"</strong>, <strong>"no upfront capital expenditure"</strong>, <strong>"pay only for
what you consume"</strong> — all of these are pointing at <strong>CapEx becoming OpEx</strong>,
and never at economies of scale or agility, which are different advantages.
</Callout>

**Total cost of ownership (TCO)** is the name for the honest version of this comparison: every
cost of running the thing over its whole life, not just its purchase price. It is the only
comparison worth making, and the reason the two Brightwell spreadsheets disagreed.

<Callout type="gotcha">
The exam will not let you conclude "the cloud is always cheaper", and neither should you. A
workload that is <strong>completely flat, fully utilised and long-lived</strong> can genuinely
be cheaper to own — that is why AWS itself sells Savings Plans and Dedicated Hosts. The
cloud's economic advantage grows with <strong>how much your demand varies</strong> and how
uncertain your future is. Brightwell's win came from a lumpy, uncertain estate, not from AWS
being inherently cheap.
</Callout>

## The four levers that decide whether you actually save

Migrating does not save money by itself. Lifting 187 servers onto identically-sized EC2
instances usually costs *more* than the data centre did, and this surprises people every year.
Four things turn it around, and they are exactly what task 1.4 of the blueprint asks about.

### 1 · Right-sizing

The single biggest lever, and the one most often skipped.

On-premises you sized every server for its peak load, plus three years of growth, plus a
margin — because getting it wrong meant a twelve-week procurement. That logic is now
obsolete: on AWS you can change an instance type in minutes, so **sizing for today is not
risky any more**.

Most rehosted servers are two to four times larger than the workload needs. **Compute
Optimizer** and **Cost Explorer's rightsizing recommendations** watch actual utilisation and
tell you which ones. Do this once, three months after a migration, and it is frequently the
largest single saving available.

### 2 · Automation

Anything done by hand costs staff time every time it happens, and staff time is the cost that
never appears on the AWS bill but always appears in the business.

Instances that start and stop on a schedule cost nothing overnight. Environments built from a
CloudFormation template cost one afternoon instead of a week, and stop drifting apart.
Auto Scaling gives capacity back without anyone deciding to. Every one of these converts a
recurring human cost into a one-off engineering cost.

### 3 · Managed services

Running your own MySQL on EC2 means you patch it, back it up, test the restores, configure
replication, and get paged when it fails. **Amazon RDS** does all of that for a higher per-hour
price than the raw instance.

Compare only the per-hour prices and the managed service looks like a bad deal. Compare the
**total** cost — including the engineer's week and the risk of a restore nobody has tested —
and it rarely is. This applies the same way to **ECS and EKS** over self-managed clusters, and
**DynamoDB** over a self-run database.

<Callout type="cost">
The phrase to listen for is <strong>"reduce operational overhead"</strong>. It appears
constantly in exam questions, and it almost always means: choose the <strong>managed</strong>
service, even though its sticker price is higher. Operational overhead is a cost; the exam
treats it as one.
</Callout>

### 4 · Licensing

Software licences do not disappear when the hardware does, and they are examinable in two forms:

- **Bring Your Own License (BYOL)** — you already own a licence and want to keep using it.
  Where a licence is tied to *physical* CPU sockets or cores, you may need **Dedicated Hosts**,
  which give you visibility of the physical server. It is the most expensive way to buy EC2 and
  is chosen for exactly this reason.
- **License-included** — AWS bundles the licence into the hourly price. Windows Server or SQL
  Server on EC2, or a commercial engine on RDS. No licence to track, no compliance audit to
  fail, and you stop paying the moment you stop the instance.

**License Manager** exists to track all of this and stop you breaching terms by accident.

Brightwell went with the finance director's model, and it turned out to be roughly right — but
not for the reason anybody argued about in the meeting.

The saving did not come from EC2 being cheaper than their servers, because it was not. It came
from right-sizing 187 machines that had been specified for a peak in 2019, switching off the
development estate at night and at weekends, and no longer employing two people to look after
hardware.

Which is the actual lesson, and it is worth carrying into the exam and the job in the same
sentence: **the cloud does not save you money. It gives you the ability to stop paying for
things you are not using — and that ability does nothing at all until somebody uses it.**
