## The problem

Aldgate Health cannot move everything, and never will.

The MRI scanner writes to a machine in the basement, over a protocol designed in the 1990s, and
the manufacturer's support contract voids if anything about that changes. The patient
administration system is licensed per physical socket on hardware the trust owns. And a
regulator has views about where certain records live.

Meanwhile the things that *should* be in the cloud obviously should: the eleven-year archive of
scans nobody looks at, the analytics, the disaster recovery copy, the burst capacity for
research workloads.

So the answer is both, permanently — and the exam is unusually clear that this is a legitimate,
finished architecture rather than a migration somebody abandoned halfway.

**Storage Gateway** is the piece people meet least and misidentify most. It is not a migration
tool — that is **DataSync** for network transfers and **Snowball** when the wire would take
months. Storage Gateway is for the *permanent* case: an on-premises application that must keep
seeing a normal file share or iSCSI volume, while the bytes actually live in S3 with a local
cache for recent files. Aldgate's acquisition workstation writes to what it believes is a
Windows share; the objects land in S3; nobody rewrote a 1990s protocol.

<Callout type="exam" title="Three services, three completely different jobs">
This trio is confused constantly, and the distinction is about <em>time</em>:
<br />· <strong>Snowball</strong> — a one-off bulk move where the network would take weeks or
months. A physical box arrives by courier.
<br />· <strong>DataSync</strong> — repeated or ongoing transfer over a network link that is
perfectly adequate.
<br />· <strong>Storage Gateway</strong> — <strong>not a move at all.</strong> A permanent hybrid
arrangement where on-premises systems keep local access to cloud-backed storage.
</Callout>

**Why not just move the archive with a script?** Because the requirement is not "get the files
to S3 once". It is that the scanner keeps writing, forever, to something that looks exactly like
what it has always written to. A one-off migration solves a different problem than the one
Aldgate has.

## Roughly what it costs

Hybrid is the most expensive pattern here, and the costs are unusually lumpy.

| Line | Rough monthly cost |
| --- | --- |
| Direct Connect port | tens to hundreds of pounds by capacity, plus installation |
| Direct Connect data transfer out | cheaper per GB than internet egress |
| Site-to-Site VPN | roughly $0.05 per tunnel-hour, so tens of pounds |
| Storage Gateway | the appliance is free; you pay for the S3 underneath |
| S3 + Glacier for the archive | falls sharply once lifecycle rules run |
| **Shape** | **a real fixed floor, then cheap storage** |

The Direct Connect port is a genuine fixed cost, and the honest justification for it is rarely
money — it is latency consistency, regulatory posture, or removing the public internet from a
clinical path. If a question offers Direct Connect as the *cheap* answer, that is usually the
distractor.

It is worth ending this section here rather than on something more modern, because this is where
a large number of real careers are actually spent.

The serverless architectures earlier are how new things get built, and they are a pleasure. But
walk into a hospital, a bank, a manufacturer or a government department and you will find
something that looks like this diagram: a building full of equipment that cannot move, joined by
a circuit to a VPC full of things that should never have been in a building.

Being able to look at that and see a *design* rather than a mess is most of what makes somebody
useful in their first year.
