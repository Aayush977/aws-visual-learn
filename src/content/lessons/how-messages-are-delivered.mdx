## Fourteen texts before breakfast

Aldgate Health send appointment reminders overnight. A queue holds the reminders, a worker
picks them up and hands them to an SMS provider, and for two years it had been the least
interesting system in the building.

Then one Tuesday a patient received the same reminder **fourteen times between 02:00 and
06:00**. Fourteen identical texts about a dermatology appointment, arriving roughly every
seventeen minutes, through the night.

The complaint reached a team who checked the obvious things. There was no loop in the code. The
reminder existed once in the database. The worker had not crashed and restarted — its logs
showed it processing the message calmly, fourteen separate times, each one apparently the first.

The SMS provider had been slow that night. Not down — slow. Each send was taking a little over
half a minute instead of the usual two seconds.

**Nothing was broken.** The queue was doing precisely what a queue is specified to do, and
every one of those fourteen texts was correct behaviour by a system nobody in the room fully
understood.

## Receiving is not removing

Here is the sentence the whole lesson rests on:

> **Taking a message off a queue does not delete it.** You receive a *copy*. The message is
> hidden for a while, and if you do not explicitly delete it before that window closes, it
> comes back and somebody else gets it.

That is not a quirk of SQS. It is the only safe design: if receiving deleted the message, then
a consumer that crashed halfway through would take the work with it, permanently. The queue
protects against a dying consumer by assuming *every* consumer might be dying, and taking the
message back if it does not hear otherwise.

Aldgate's worker was taking 35 seconds to send a text. Their visibility timeout was 30. So at
second 30 the message reappeared, the worker picked it up again, sent the text again, and the
cycle repeated until the message finally aged out.

## The SQS settings that decide your behaviour

**Visibility timeout** — how long a received message stays hidden. Default 30 seconds, maximum
12 hours. It must be **longer than your slowest successful processing time**, or you have built
Aldgate's system. For a Lambda consumer the guidance is at least six times the function
timeout.

**Message retention** — how long an undelivered message survives in the queue at all. Four days
by default, up to fourteen. This is your margin when a consumer has been down over a weekend.

**maxReceiveCount and the dead-letter queue** — a redrive policy saying "after this many
receives, move it aside". Without one, a message that always fails is retried until it expires,
burning capacity the whole time. **A queue without a dead-letter queue is a queue that hides
its failures.**

**Long polling** — the setting almost everyone should change. Short polling returns
immediately, empty or not, so an idle consumer spends its life asking a question and being told
no, at a cost. Long polling waits up to 20 seconds for a message to appear. Fewer empty
responses, lower cost, and lower latency when work does arrive.

**Message size** — up to 256 KB. Anything larger belongs in S3 with the message carrying a
pointer to it. Queues are for notifications about data, not for the data.

<Callout type="gotcha">
The fix for Aldgate looks like "raise the visibility timeout", and that is necessary but not
sufficient. Raise it to 5 minutes and a night where the provider takes 6 minutes produces the
same bug, just more rarely and more confusingly. <strong>The durable fix is an idempotent
consumer</strong> — one that records the reminder id before sending and refuses to send it
twice. Then a redelivery is harmless, whatever the timeout is set to.
</Callout>

### Standard and FIFO

Two kinds of queue, and the difference is what they promise.

- **Standard** — effectively unlimited throughput, best-effort ordering, and
  **at-least-once** delivery. A message can arrive more than once even when nothing has gone
  wrong. This is the default and the right answer for most work.
- **FIFO** — strict ordering within a **message group**, and exactly-once *processing* through
  deduplication. Lower throughput than standard, though a high-throughput mode exists. Use it
  when order genuinely matters — a sequence of updates to one account, where applying them out
  of order produces a wrong answer.

The **message group id** is the part people miss: FIFO guarantees order *within* a group, not
across the queue. Use the customer id or account id as the group, and you get strict ordering
per customer while different customers still process in parallel. One group for everything
means one consumer at a time, and a queue that cannot keep up.

<Callout type="exam">
"Messages must be processed in the exact order they were sent" → <strong>FIFO queue</strong>.
"The application must handle duplicate messages" or "high throughput" with no ordering
requirement → <strong>standard queue</strong>. If both order and scale are demanded, the answer
is a FIFO queue with a sensible <strong>message group id</strong>, not a bigger consumer.
</Callout>

## SNS: push, to everyone at once

A queue holds one message for one consumer to collect. **Amazon SNS** does the opposite: a
**topic** receives a message and **pushes a copy to every subscriber immediately.**

Subscribers can be SQS queues, Lambda functions, HTTPS endpoints, email addresses, SMS numbers
or mobile push. Nobody polls — SNS delivers.

**Filter policies** are the feature worth knowing and the one most often missed. A subscription
can carry a filter on the message's attributes, so a subscriber receives only the messages it
cares about. Without filters, everyone gets everything and each consumer starts with an `if`
statement discarding most of its traffic — work you are paying for, in a place where nobody
can see it.

### Fan-out: SNS in front of SQS

The pattern that turns up constantly, and the reason these two are taught together:

**SNS topic → several SQS queues → a consumer behind each.**

SNS gives every interested system its own copy. Each queue then buffers for its own consumer, at
its own pace, with its own retries and its own dead-letter queue. A slow consumer falls behind
in its own queue without affecting anyone else, and one that is down catches up when it
returns rather than losing the events.

SNS alone cannot do that: push to an endpoint that is down retries for a while and then gives
up. Putting a queue behind each subscriber converts "delivered or lost" into "waiting".

<Callout type="exam">
"Multiple systems must each process every message, and one of them is sometimes unavailable"
is the <strong>SNS fan-out to SQS</strong> pattern, every time. SNS on its own is the answer
only when the subscriber is always available, or when the destination is a human — an email or
a text.
</Callout>

## EventBridge: routing on what the event says

**Amazon EventBridge** is an event bus. Events arrive; **rules** inspect their *content* and
send matching ones to targets.

Three things it does that SNS does not:

- **It already receives events from AWS itself.** An instance changing state, a deployment
  finishing, an S3 object being created, a Config rule going non-compliant — these land on the
  default bus whether you asked or not. Reacting to what AWS did is EventBridge's home ground.
- **It matches on content, not just attributes.** A rule can select events where the detail
  says `status: FAILED` and the amount is over a threshold, and route those somewhere
  different from everything else.
- **It can archive and replay.** Events can be kept and re-delivered later — which means a
  consumer you deploy next month can be fed last month's events, and a bug that dropped events
  can be recovered from rather than apologised for.

It also takes events from **third-party SaaS products** and can run **scheduled** events, which
is the modern answer to a cron job with nowhere to live.

The trade is latency and throughput: SNS is the leaner, faster pipe. EventBridge is the one
that understands what it is carrying.

### Lab 1 — fan-out and message filtering

A ride-completion event has to reach three services that each want something different: a
customer notification service, a customer accounting service, and an "extraordinary rides"
service that only cares about rides above a certain fare or distance.

That last one is the reason this lab exists. It is the case for **filter policies** — the
third service subscribes to the same topic as the other two and receives only the messages it
wants, rather than receiving everything and discarding most of it in an `if` statement.

**What it teaches, mapped back:**

| In the lab | In this lesson |
|---|---|
| Creating the SNS topic and adding Lambda subscriptions | *SNS: push, to everyone at once* |
| A subscription filter on the extraordinary-rides service | Filter policies, and the work they save |
| Three subscribers on one topic | Each subscriber getting its own copy |

### Lab 2 — topic-queue chaining

The same three services, with an SQS queue inserted between the topic and each one.

The workshop's own justification is worth quoting almost directly, because it is the exam
answer: one of these services **could be taken offline for maintenance**, or could hit an
exception, and either way it would miss topic messages. Buffering each subscriber behind its
own queue means nothing is lost while it is away.

This is the **SNS → SQS fan-out** section of this lesson, and it is also, almost word for
word, the second quiz question above. Doing the lab and then re-reading that question is a
good way to find out whether you learned the pattern or memorised the answer.

The lab also makes the second, less obvious point: a queue in front of a subscriber is a
**buffering load balancer**. Because each message goes to exactly one consumer process, you
can scale that subscriber out and the messages distribute themselves — and a scaling event
loses nothing, because the queue is holding everything until a consumer is ready.

**What it teaches, mapped back:**

| In the lab | In this lesson |
|---|---|
| Adding an SQS queue between topic and consumer | Why SNS alone converts "delivered" into "lost" |
| SQS as an event source for Lambda | Event source mapping — Lambda polls the queue |
| Scaling consumers behind one queue | One message, one consumer |

### Step by step

1. Start with **Lab 0**, the prerequisites — it sets up the shared environment the others use.
2. Do **Lab 1**, then **Lab 2**. They are the two this lesson covers and they build on the same
   story, so doing them in order is worth it even though the workshop says each is independent.
3. Labs 3 and 4 (scatter-gather, and orchestration with Step Functions) go beyond this lesson.
   They are good, and they are the natural next thing once queues make sense.
4. **Do the clean-up section.** Each lab has one, and they exist because this workshop leaves
   topics, queues and functions behind.

### Break it on purpose

The workshop is a happy path. These are the experiments that turn it into understanding:

1. **Receive a message and do not delete it.** Use the console's *Poll for messages*, watch the
   message appear, then walk away. Poll again after the visibility timeout and it is back. This
   is the single most important behaviour in this lesson and it takes ninety seconds to see.
2. **Watch the receive count.** The console shows *Approximate receive count* per message.
   Poll the same message repeatedly and watch it climb toward the redrive threshold, then find
   it in the dead-letter queue.
3. **Break one subscriber only.** Point one of the three Lambda subscriptions at code that
   throws, and confirm the other two are completely unaffected. Then check its queue depth
   climbing while the others stay at zero — the isolation is the whole argument for topic-queue
   chaining.
4. **Delete a filter policy** and watch the extraordinary-rides service start receiving every
   ride. Cheap to do, and it makes the cost of *not* filtering concrete.
5. **Send the same message twice** and see that nothing stops you. Then add the idempotency
   check — record the id, return early if seen — and see that the second one does nothing.

Nobody at Aldgate had done anything wrong. They had built a queue the way queues are built,
and it worked for two years, and then a third party was slow for one night and the design
handed a patient fourteen identical texts.

The visibility timeout went up the next morning. What actually fixed it, a week later, was four
lines that wrote the reminder id to a table before the send and checked it on the way in — so
the fifteenth delivery of the same message does nothing at all.

That is the habit worth taking from this lesson. **A message system does not promise to deliver
your message once. It promises not to lose it** — and those are very different promises, with
the gap between them filled by code you have to write.
